### **Introduction** 

Spectrum management operations enable and support the Army warfighting functions and are essential to commanders’ ability to exercise command and control throughout their assigned areas. Spectrum management enables electromagnetic spectrum access to support spectrum-dependent devices throughout a unit’s assigned area through frequency deconfliction, and resolution of electromagnetic interference. 

Spectrum managers in the cyberspace electromagnetic activities section deconflict frequency use for electromagnetic warfare, providing awareness of potential spectrum conflict between spectrum-dependent devices and reducing the risk of frequency fratricide. The cyberspace electromagnetic activities sections integrate spectrum management and electromagnetic warfare through the cyberspace electromagnetic activities working group and integrate electromagnetic spectrum operations through the joint electromagnetic spectrum operations cell. 

Spectrum managers encounter numerous challenges managing the use of the electromagnetic spectrum. These challenges come from competing spectrum-dependent devices that are either friendly, threat, or neutral. Spectrum managers use various spectrum management tools to conduct spectrum management operations. These tools allow the spectrum manager create products and input during the military decisionmaking process and cyberspace electromagnetic activities working group. 

This publication contains three chapters and five appendixes. 

- **Chapter 1** provides an overview of spectrum management operations, states the objectives, and describes spectrum management operations core functions. 

- **Chapter 2** discusses spectrum managers’ support and input to the military decision-making process and briefly describes the common operational picture. 

- **Chapter 3** links Army spectrum management operations to the warfighting functions, describes how spectrum management operations support and enable commanders’ efforts as they exercise command and control. 

- **Appendix A** provides basic electromagnetic physics and the underlying principles of the electromagnetic spectrum. 

- **Appendix B** provides spectrum management tools, including their compatibilities and capabilities to meet spectrum management operations critical tasks. These systems are in many cases linked and accessible through Nonclassified Internet Protocol Router Network and SECRET Internet Protocol Router Network. 

- **Appendix C** introduces the spectrum management life cycle. This process serves as a guide to follow in establishing a functional and efficient spectrum management program life cycle. 

- **Appendix D** provides the reader with an overview of the military time zone designators for civilian and military use. 

- **Appendix E** describes spectrum information and products necessary at the corps and joint task force levels.

##### **Chapter 1** 

### **Overview** 

This chapter provides an overview of operational environments and spectrum management operations. It describes various challenges spectrum managers may encounter while operating in a congested and contested environment. It also provides an overview of the spectrum management operations process. This chapter describes the core functions related to spectrum management operations. 

##### **SECTION I – OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT AND THREAT** 

1-1. This section describes the operational environment and the effects of operating in a congested and contested environment. It also details environmental considerations that should be considered while managing the electromagnetic spectrum. This section discusses enemies, adversaries, and peer threats. 

#### **OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT** 

1-2. An _operational environment_ is the aggregate of the conditions, circumstances, and influences that affect the employment of capabilities and bear on the commander’s decisions (JP 3-0). The operational environment encompasses the air, land, maritime, space, and cyberspace domains. For the Army, the _cyberspace domain_ is the interdependent networks of information technology infrastructures and resident data, including the Internet, telecommunication networks, computer systems, embedded processors and controllers, and relevant portions of the electromagnetic spectrum (FM 3-0). The electromagnetic spectrum is crucial in all domains, enabling communication and weapon systems capabilities when properly managed. 

##### **CONGESTED AND CONTESTED ENVIRONMENT** 

1-3. Gaining and maintaining control of the electromagnetic spectrum is critical for the commander. All forces and supporting agencies depend on the electromagnetic spectrum to execute operations in the air, land, maritime, space, and cyberspace domains. Within the electromagnetic spectrum, joint forces compete with civil agencies, commercial entities, allied forces, and threats for the use of a common electromagnetic spectrum resource. This demand for electromagnetic spectrum results in a congested and contested environment that affects operations across all domains and functions. 

###### **Congested** 

1-4. The electromagnetic spectrum congestion results from multiple spectrum-dependent devices attempting to simultaneously use the same portions of the spectrum. Congestion can lead to the operational failure of systems during critical missions due to electromagnetic interference. The Army shares spectrum-related resources with other Services, civilian counterparts, friendly forces, and threats. Due to many spectrumdependent devices using the electromagnetic spectrum, portions of the spectrum may be unavailable. 

1-5. In the past, the bulk of spectrum management comprised networked communications emitters and combat net radio networks. Today, the tactical environment includes many spectrum-dependent devices operating throughout the electromagnetic spectrum across the battlefield. The key to sound spectrum management is understanding all systems in the assigned area for deconfliction. 

1-6. The G-6 or S-6 spectrum manager coordinates with all electromagnetic spectrum users within a given assigned area to identify all requirements for spectrum access. The spectrum manager uses this information to conduct frequency deconfliction. The G-6 or S-6 spectrum manager also maintains a database of all known emitters and receivers in the assigned area. The use of this database is to identify and prioritize competing systems for frequency assignments. ###### **Contested** 

1-13. Threats increasingly contest the friendly use of the electromagnetic spectrum. Enemies and adversaries may deliberately attempt to deny the friendly use of spectrum-dependent devices in all domains. Due to heavy joint reliance on advanced communications systems, such an attack may be a central element of a threat’s antiaccess and area denial strategy. This may require a higher degree of protection for friendly command and control systems. 

1-14. Threats have demonstrated an increased technological capability that challenges U.S. access to the electromagnetic spectrum. U.S. military communications and information networks are priority targets for threats to disrupt communications. Threats understand the extent of U.S. forces’ reliance on the electromagnetic spectrum. Commanders should expect that in future conflicts, threats will contest the electromagnetic spectrum to deny operational access and diminish the effectiveness of U.S. and allied forces. 

1-15. Integrating signal support with cyberspace, electromagnetic warfare, and intelligence operations is critical for achieving and maintaining freedom of action in the electromagnetic spectrum. These capabilities also enable the ability to weaken or prevent the threat’s ability to carry out successful operations. Threat actions can lead to reduced capabilities of U.S. forces and joint and multinational information systems. Synchronizing capabilities across multiple domains and warfighting functions can maximize their inherently complementary effects throughout the electromagnetic spectrum. 

##### **OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT CONSIDERATIONS** 

1-16. Reliable access to the electromagnetic spectrum is critical in cyberspace operations, electromagnetic warfare, and signal support. Factors within the operational environment can impact the ability to access the electromagnetic spectrum. Understanding how these factors can impact access to the electronic spectrum and how to mitigate the effects are required to conduct spectrum management operations effectively. Factors within the operational environment that can impact access to the electromagnetic spectrum include— 

- Terrain. 

- Weather—atmospheric and space. 

- Light and illumination. 

- Civil considerations. 

###### **Terrain** 

1-17. The terrain within the electromagnetic operational environment can significantly impact the propagation of electromagnetic signals. Different types of terrain, such as mountains, forests, or urban areas, can affect signal strength and quality. Understanding how terrain features like hills, buildings, or dense vegetation can obstruct, reflect, or attenuate signals is essential for effective communications and electromagnetic warfare operations. 

###### **Weather** 

1-18. Weather conditions within the operational environment can greatly influence the use of the electromagnetic spectrum. Factors such as rain, fog, snowstorms, or even extreme heat can affect the performance of electromagnetic systems. Rainfall can attenuate radio waves, while atmospheric interference caused by temperature inversions can distort signals. 

1-19. Space weather can also have a significant impact on the electromagnetic operational environment. The sun is the primary source of space weather, and solar flares, coronal mass ejections, and other solar phenomena can release huge amounts of electromagnetic radiation and charged particles into space. When these particles interact with the Earth’s magnetic field, they can cause disruptions to radio communications, power grids, and other electronic systems. Awareness of these weather-related challenges helps in planning and executing effective spectrum management.

###### **Light and Illumination** 

1-20. The illumination and lighting conditions in the environment play a critical role in various technologies that rely on optics and visual information, including surveillance, reconnaissance, and targeting systems. analyzing the available light and potential illumination sources in the area to ensure optimal performance and accuracy of these systems. By assessing the lighting conditions, operators can make informed decisions and adjustments to ensure that the system can effectively capture and analyze the visual information needed to achieve their objectives. 

###### **Civil Considerations** 

1-21. Civil considerations refer to elements of the operational environment that are outside military control but can impact operations. These factors may include civilian infrastructure such as roads and buildings, public communication systems, and potential electromagnetic interference from civilian sources. Local regulations and restrictions must be considered to ensure that operations comply with the law and without infringing on civilian rights. 

#### **THREATS** 

1-22. A _threat_ is any combination of actors, entities, or forces that have the capability and intent to harm U.S. forces, U.S. national interests, or the homeland (ADP 3-0). Threats can be categorized as enemies, adversaries, or insider threats. An _enemy_ is a party identified as hostile against which the use of force is authorized (ADP 3-0). An _adversary_ is a party acknowledged as potentially hostile to a friendly party and against which the use of force may be envisaged (JP 3-0). The distinction between an enemy and an adversary may depend on context and the category in the competition continuum. 

1-23. Threat actors can disrupt, destroy, or intercept the flow of information while operating from the other side of the world. Threats may include individuals or groups of individuals, organized, or not organized. Army forces are organized, trained, and equipped primarily for large-scale combat operations against peer threats. A peer threat is an adversary or enemy with capabilities and capacity to oppose U.S. forces across multiple domains worldwide or in a specific region where they enjoy a position of relative advantage (FM 3-0). Threats may use the spectrum to locate, disrupt, or interfere with communications, navigation, and other critical systems, making it essential for spectrum managers to understand and monitor the electromagnetic spectrum. 

1-24. Spectrum managers must understand what capabilities peer threats can employ to degrade or destroy their networks. Those capabilities include— 

- Maneuver. 

- Manned and unmanned aircraft systems. 

- Intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance. 

- Artillery. 

- Long-range precision fires. 

- Electromagnetic attack. 

- Malicious cyberspace activity. 

- Space based capabilities.

##### **SECTION II – THE ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM** 

1-25. The electromagnetic spectrum consists of the entire range of wavelengths or frequencies of electromagnetic radiation extending from gamma rays to the longest radio waves and including visible light. It is a maneuver space essential for facilitating control within the operational environment and impacts all portions of the operational environment and military operations. The electromagnetic spectrum is a vital warfighting resource that requires the same planning and management as other critical resources such as fuel, water, and ammunition. The Army is dependent upon the use of the electromagnetic spectrum at all echelons.

#### **ELECTROMAGNETIC ENVIRONMENT** 

1-26. The _electromagnetic environment_ is the resulting product of power and time distribution in various frequency ranges of the radiated or conducted electromagnetic emission levels encountered by military forces, systems, or platforms while performing an assigned mission within its intended operational environment (JP 3-85). The electromagnetic environment consists of all spectrum emissions within an assigned area. #### **ELECTROMAGNETIC OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT** 

1-27. The _electromagnetic operational environment_ is a composite of the actual and potential electromagnetic energy radiation, conditions, circumstances, and influences that affect the employment of capabilities and the decisions of the commander (JP 3-85). The electromagnetic operational environment is the portion of the electromagnetic environment where the spectrum manager conducts spectrum management operations and is the authoritative source of spectrum-use information. The electromagnetic operational environment includes the background electromagnetic environment and all friendly threat (to the extent available), and neutral spectrum-dependent devices within an assigned area. The spectrum manager provides input to the electromagnetic order of battle.

###### **Evaluating and Mitigating Electromagnetic Environmental Effects** 

1-37. . Devices operating within the electromagnetic spectrum are inherently susceptible to electromagnetic environmental effects. It is essential for spectrum managers to identify and evaluate the potential critical impacts of these effects in order to effectively manage and mitigate the hazards associated with electromagnetic radiation during operations. Mitigation strategies may include coordination, deconfliction, emission control, and antenna placement. Refer to DODI 3222.03 for more information on the DOD electromagnetic environmental effects program. 

###### **Managing Frequency Records and Databases** 

1-38. Spectrum-use databases play a crucial role in managing the spectrum, as they help maintain the continuous and efficient use of the electromagnetic spectrum in various operational environments. These databases contain essential information about spectrum use for all devices that rely on it, including those used by friendly, civilian, threat (if available), and neutral entities. They encompass both communication and noncommunication emitters. To ensure the accuracy and usefulness of this data for operational units, it is vital to coordinate with the organizations responsible for maintaining spectrum management databases. Some commonly used databases in the electromagnetic spectrum domain are— 

- The Joint Equipment, Tactical, and Space Database. 

- The Equipment Location-Certification Information Database. 

- The Government Master File Database. 

- The Frequency Resource Record System Database. 

- The Host Nation Spectrum Worldwide Database Online 

**_Note_ .** See appendix B for more information about electromagnetic spectrum-use databases. 

###### **Deconflicting Frequencies** 

1-39. _Frequency deconfliction_ is a systematic management procedure to coordinate the use of the electromagnetic spectrum for operations, communications, and intelligence functions (JP 3-85). Frequency deconfliction is one element of electromagnetic spectrum management and applies practices to minimize or prevent spectrum-dependent devices from causing electromagnetic interference. It is easy to confuse electromagnetic interference mitigation with frequency deconfliction. The main difference is that frequency deconfliction occurs during a mission’s planning phase, while electromagnetic interference mitigation occurs during mission execution. 

###### **Frequency Interference Mitigation** 

1-40. Mitigation and resolution of electromagnetic interference is the process of reducing or eliminating disruptive electromagnetic fields that can interfere with electronic devices. Electromagnetic interference from manmade sources may include power lines, generators, alarm systems, electric railroads, and fluorescent lighting. Spectrum managers advise users to isolate spectrum-dependent devices from manmade interference sources. 

###### **_Interference Resolution_** 

1-41. Spectrum managers perform interference resolution at the echelon receiving the interference. Interference is the radiation, emission, or indication of electromagnetic energy, intentionally or unintentionally, causing degradation, disruption, or complete obstruction of the designated function of the electronic equipment affected. The spectrum manager should use available near-real-time monitoring and analysis capabilities to aid in interference resolution. The reporting end user assists the spectrum manager in tracking, evaluating, and resolving interference. See appendix B for further information about frequency interference resolution and reporting.

###### **_Frequency Fratricide_** 

1-42. Frequency fratricide is the unintentional interruption of friendly frequencies by a friendly emitter. The cyberspace electromagnetic activities (CEMA) spectrum manager deconflicts the spectrum, identifies conflicts, and advises the cyber electromagnetic warfare officer about measures to prevent or mitigate possible frequency fratricide. The CEMA spectrum manager works to mitigate frequency fratricide and potential electromagnetic interference during the planning phase of cyber and electromagnetic warfare operations. Frequency fratricide can cause many problems for operations, and prevention is critical to mission success. ###### **Joint Coordination** 

1-60. Joint electromagnetic spectrum coordination establishes the essential framework for effectively prioritizing, integrating, and harmonizing operations within the electromagnetic spectrum. This coordination encompasses several components, including the submission procedures for joint spectrum interference resolution reporting, routing procedures, electromagnetic spectrum operational environment monitoring, and JRFL development. ###### **_Joint Restricted Frequency List_** 

1-61. The JRFL is an electromagnetic spectrum coordination measure that operational, intelligence, and support elements use to identify the level of protection desired for various networks and frequencies. The _joint restricted frequency list_ is a time and geographically oriented listing of TABOO, protected, and GUARDED frequencies. It is limited to the minimum frequencies necessary for friendly forces to achieve objectives (JP 3-85). Units may not jam frequencies listed on the JRFL. The JEMSOC, with collaborative input from the J-2, J-3, and J-6, develop the JRFL. Upon approval by the J-3, the JEMSOC publishes the JRFL and distributes it to subordinate unit spectrum managers. 

1-62. The purpose of the JRFL is to protect high-priority nets, bands, and frequencies from friendly electromagnetic attacks when possible; however, the concern of the spectrum manager is to ensure that all friendly systems can operate unimpaired. They accomplish this by adding the offending jammer to a database and using spectrum management techniques such as changing frequencies, changing assignments, or moving to an unaffected area to mitigate the interference. The spectrum manager has tools that can identify potential frequency fratricide. See appendix B for more information about the JRFL. 

1-63. Adherence to the JRFL does not deconflict all frequency issues. The JRFL does not provide communications planners with frequencies electromagnetic attack systems transmit or the technical information needed to deconflict electromagnetic attacks from friendly operations, including lower echelon maneuver forces. Spectrum management tools identify potential interference and frequency conflicts during mission planning reducing frequency fratricide. Refer to CJCSM 3320.01D for more information about JRFL development.

### **Spectrum Physics** 

This appendix describes the physics of the radio frequency spectrum. A basic understanding of the underlying principles of radio frequency energy is necessary for the execution of spectrum management operations. 

#### **RADIO FREQUENCY** 

A-1. RF communications, based on the laws of physics, describes the behavior of electromagnetic energy waves. RF communications work by creating electromagnetic waves at a source and being able to receive those electromagnetic waves at a particular destination. These electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light. The wavelength of an electromagnetic signal is inversely proportional to the frequency, the shorter the wavelength. 

A-2. Frequency measurements are expressed in hertz (Hz) or cycles per second. Typically, radio frequency measurements are in kilohertz (kHz) or thousands of cycles per second, megahertz (MHz) or millions of cycles per second, or gigahertz (GHz) or billions of cycles per second. The wavelength for a device utilizing a frequency in the MHz range is longer than a frequency in a GHz range. In general, signals with longer wavelengths travel a greater distance and penetrate objects through and around better than signals with shorter wavelengths. 

A-3. Waveforms are patterns of electrical energy over time. A sine wave is the fundamental building block of electricity and other energy types. A sine wave mathematically defines a natural action describing a harmonic alternating event. 

A-4. The wavelength is the distance from one crest or trough on a wave to the same point on the preceding crest or trough. A-7. Harmonics develop into currents and voltages with frequencies that are multiples of the fundamental frequency. Harmonic signals that fall within the passband of a receiver with a signal level of sufficient amplitude can degrade the performance of the receiver. Receivers live under constant bombardment of signals that enter through the antenna port. Some of these signals quickly attenuate due to front-end filtering, also called pre-selection. 

A-8. Intermodulation occurs when multiple signals reach a non-linear element, such as a detector, mixer, or amplifier, and are mixed. Whenever two signals mix, the introduction of two additional signals result as the sum and difference of the original frequencies. This process is often intentional as in the case of mixing a frequency with the intermediate frequency in a system to produce the desired operating signal. Harmonics of the original two frequencies are still present, but most occur well outside the passband of the RF and intermediate frequency filters and cause no problems. The harmonics that tend to create the most problems are the odd-order products. For example, if a 50 MHz mixing frequency combines with a 98 MHz intermediate frequency to produce the desired transmission signal of 148 MHz, this is very close to the third order harmonic of 50 MHz (150 MHz) and may cause interference at the desired frequency. Channelized communications systems tend to suffer more from these issues due to uniform channel spacing.

#### **OVERVIEW** 

B-1. Spectrum management tools are used to plan, manage, and distribute frequencies for communications networks and spectrum-dependent devices. ### **Spectrum Management Life Cycle** 

The Army spectrum management life cycle guides establishing a functional and efficient spectrum management program. The life cycle encompasses the complete process of providing spectrum management support to the commander and applies to all spectrum managers regardless of duty location. The Army spectrum management life cycle mirrors the joint task force life cycle. 

#### **ACTIVITIES OF THE SPECTRUM MANAGEMENT LIFE CYCLE** 

C-1. The spectrum management life cycle consists of 12 activities that enhance spectrum management operations. It is optional to conduct life cycle activities as presented. Some spectrum management life cycle activities happen simultaneously, while others happen only in succession. Listed below are the 12 activities of the spectrum management life cycle— 

- Activity 1—Define command-specific policy and guidance. 

- Activity 2—Gather requirements. 

- Activity 3—Develop an electromagnetic spectrum requirements summary. 

- Activity 4—Define the electromagnetic operational environment. 

- Activity 5—Obtain spectrum resources. 

- Activity 6—Develop a spectrum management plan. 

- Activity 7—Nominate and assign frequencies. 

- Activity 8—Generate joint communication-electronics operating instructions. 

- Activity 9—Develop JRFL. 

- Activity 10—Perform electromagnetic warfare deconfliction. 

- Activity 11—Resolve interference. 

- Activity 12—Report interference.

C-2. ##### **TYPES OF JAMMING SIGNALS**

- Spot jamming—power concentrated toward one channel or frequency. 

- Barrage jamming—power spread over several frequencies or channels simultaneously. 

C-47. Jamming can be difficult and sometimes impossible to detect. Users of spectrum-dependent devices have the potential of being jammed and should be trained to recognize jamming. The two types of jamming most encountered are obvious and subtle jamming. Obvious jamming is usually very simple to detect. When experiencing a jamming incident, it is more important to recognize and overcome the incident than to identify it formally. ##### **RECOGNIZING JAMMING** 

C-48. Equipment operators must be able to recognize jamming. Threat jammers may employ obvious or subtle jamming techniques. In addition, interference caused by sources having nothing to do with threat jamming may be the source. Jammers affect receivers and do not affect transmitters. 

C-49. The following may cause prohibitive electromagnetic interference: 

- Other radios (friendly and threat)—may be intentional or unintentional. 

- Other electronic, electric, or electromechanical equipment. 

- Atmospheric conditions. 

- Malfunction of the radio. 

- Improper operation of the radio. 

- Combination of any of the above. 

###### **Internal or External Interference** 

C-50. The two sources of interference are internal and external. If the interference or suspected jamming remains after grounding or disconnecting the antenna, the disturbance is most likely internal and caused by a malfunction of the radio. The operator should contact maintenance personnel to assist in troubleshooting. Further examinations could reveal external interference from threat jamming or unintentional interference. 

###### **Jamming or Unintentional Interference** 

C-51. The battlefield is so crowded with radios and other electronic equipment that some unintentional interference is virtually unavoidable. Static electricity produced by atmospheric conditions can negatively affect radio communications. Unintentional interference usually travels a short distance, and a search of the immediate area may reveal the source of this type of interference. Moving the receiving antenna a short distance may cause noticeable variations in the strength of the interfering signal. These variations usually indicate unintentional interference. Conversely, little or no variation may indicate inadvertent friendly or

##### **OVERCOMING JAMMING** 

C-53. Threats continually strive to perfect and use new and more confusing forms of jamming. Equipment operators must be increasingly alert to the possibility of jamming. Training and experience are essential tools operators must use to determine when a particular signal is jamming. Exposure to the effects of jamming during training events or actual situations is invaluable. The ability to recognize jamming is essential because jamming is a problem that requires action. 

C-54. Operators continue to operate affected equipment if jamming does occur. Usually, threat jamming involves a period of jamming followed by a brief listening period. The threat will attempt to determine how effective jamming has been. What the victim does during this short listening period tells the jammer how effective jamming has been. 

C-55. If the operation continues as before the jamming began, the threat may assume that jamming has not been effective. If the threat finds users discussing the jamming over the radio or they shut down operation entirely, the threat may assume that jamming has been effective. Because the threat jammer monitors operations, unless otherwise ordered, operators never cease operation or disclose adversely effects. Normal operation should continue even when degraded by jamming. Methods to overcome jamming include— 

- Improving the signal-to-jamming ratio. 

- Repositioning antennas. 

- Establishing a retransmission station. 

- Using an alternate route for communication. 

- Changing frequencies. 

###### **Improve the Signal-to-Jamming Ratio** 

C-56. The signal-to-jamming ratio compares the desired signal’s strength to the interference signal. The desired signal is the frequency users want to receive, while the jamming signal refers to any unwanted or unidentified interference. If the desired signal is stronger than the jamming signal, it will not significantly degrade the quality of the desired signal. 

C-57. Users experiencing jamming may take various steps to improve the signal-to-jamming ratio. Adjusting the receiver to ensure frequency tuning is as precise as possible to the desired incoming signal may improve the signal-to-jamming ratio. Additional techniques to improve signal-to-jamming ratio include— 

- Adjusting the radio frequency bandwidth if possible. 

- Adjusting the gain or volume control. 

- Fine-tuning the frequency. 

- Increasing transmitter power output. 

C-58. Increasing the transmitter’s power output by emitting the desired signal improves the signal-tojamming ratio. The transmitter must be set on something less than full power when jamming increases the power output at the time of jamming. Using low power as a preventive technique depends on the threat’s inability to detect radio transmissions. Once the threat begins jamming radios, the risk of exposure increases. 

###### **Reposition Antennas** 

C-59. Users experiencing jamming should ensure antennas are in the best position to receive the desired signal. Additional techniques to improve incoming signal strength regarding antenna position include—

### **Military Time Zone Designators** 

Spectrum managers communicate with users across different time zones and provide commanders with operational times established during missions. Support for airborne operations provides the most extensive use of military time zones during flight missions. Knowledge of global military time zone differences for spectrum managers is vital. Spectrum managers can prepare briefings, conduct, and assist planning, or provide input to critical documents; accurate time zone information is essential. 

#### **MILITARY TIME** 

D-1. Military time uses the 24-hour clock beginning at midnight (0000) and ending at 2359. Military time format eliminates the need to use A.M. and P.M. designations, as regular time uses numbers 1 to 12 to identify the hours in a day. In military time, 12:00 P.M. is 1200, 1:00 P.M. is 1300, 2:00 P.M. is 1400, continuing until 11:00 P.M., which is 2300. The military uses this standard as it leaves less room for ambiguity than the 12-hour clock. The earth has 24 equally divided time zones, each with a letter designation and the military phonetic alphabet word. 

D-2. The time zone for Greenwich, England, is the letter Z, and the military phonetic word is Zulu. Since many operations coordinate across time zones, the military uses Coordinated Universal Time (formerly Greenwich Mean Time) as the standard time. The U.S. military refers to this as Zulu time and attaches the suffix Z to ensure the related time zone is clear. 

D-3. When referring to specific military time zones, users speak the letter or word attached. For example, 3:00 P.M., or 1500 Zulu time, which is written as 1500Z. 

D-4. The time zones in the United States are R, S, T, U, V, W, and X. Local time uses the letter J. The written format for 9:00 A.M. local time is 0900J and is spoken as zero nine hundred Juliet time. The L time zone designator does not equate to local time. See the time zone chart, figure D-1 on page 58 for the location of the Lima time zone. 

##### **TIME ZONE OFFSETS** 

D-5. Some countries have a 15-, 30-, or 45-minute offset from the designated time zone designator. Although located in the D time zone, local time in Afghanistan is Coordinated Universal Time +4 hours 30 minutes. An asterisk behind the affected time zone designator denotes a local time offset is in effect. See the time zone chart for affected regions. 

##### **DATE-TIME GROUP** 

D-6. Military date-time groups are indicated in the 12-character format DD####RMMMYY. The first two characters [DD] are the two-digit day of the month. The next five characters are the military time, with time zone indicator. The next three characters [MMM] are the three-letter abbreviations for the month. The final two characters [YY] are the last two digits of the year. Using this format, 1:30 P.M. Coordinated Universal Time on April 15th, 2025, would be rendered as 151330ZAPR25. 

#### **MILITARY TIME ZONE CONSIDERATIONS**

- The military observes daylight savings time when recognized by the state or country.

- The 12 time zones east of the Zulu time zone (Coordinated Universal Time), starting at the International Date Line and ending in the Pacific Ocean, are Alpha through Mike.

#### **JOINT ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM OPERATIONS CELL** 

E-5. The JEMSOC is a temporary organization that activates only for the duration of a specific joint task force operation or campaign. The JEMSOC members assigned to the joint task force’s joint cyber center of the come from the Service components of the joint task force. The lead Service of a joint task force may task its senior spectrum manager as the JEMSOC lead. Upon designation, the Service’s spectrum management office assumes the roles and responsibilities of the JEMSOC. 

E-6. Spectrum management personnel from multinational forces and other Services augment organizations designated as a JEMSOC during deliberate planning using the global force management process. Initial augmentation during crisis action planning may come from joint enablers such as the joint spectrum center or the joint electromagnetic warfare center or directly from the combatant command or J-6 joint frequency management office. Depending on the size of the force structure supported, JEMSOC end strength ranges from three to ten positions. 

E-7. The JEMSOC establishes joint task force guidance for the functions and the communicationselectronics operating instructions. The JEMSOC develops the JRFL by gathering requirements from the J-2, J-3, and J-6 for approval by the J-3. The JEMSOC has the authority to either issue frequency assignments or allotments or delegate this authority. However, the JEMSOC must ensure that components overlapping geographic areas do not assign multiple frequencies on the same RF band. 

E-8. The JEMSOC maintains the common spectrum-use database required for planning and coordinating access to the electromagnetic operational environment. This database includes spectrum use information about military, civilian, and threat forces. 

E-9. The JEMSOC ensures that assigned joint task force elements receive sufficient access to the spectrum to execute their designated missions. The JEMSOC satisfies spectrum needs and provides frequency deconfliction for all spectrum-dependent devices before assignment or allotment, including systems used by the joint task force, Service components, and multinational forces, such as the United Nations, or the North Atlantic Treaty Organization. #### **SPECTRUM MANAGEMENT SUPPORT TO DEFENSE SUPPORT OF CIVIL AUTHORITIES** 

E-11. Defense support of civil authorities encompasses all support provided by the components of the Army to civil authorities within the United States and its territories and possessions. Defense support of civil authorities includes support provided by the Active Army, Army Reserve, and the Army National Guard of the United States. Title 10, U.S. Code applies when National Guard forces are activated under federal control (Army National Guard of the United States). Title 32, U.S. Code applies to National Guard forces conducting defense support of civil authorities under the authority of their respective state (Army National Guard). Army forces conduct defense support from civil authorities in response to requests from federal, state, local, and tribal jurisdictions for domestic incidents, emergencies, disasters, designated law enforcement support, and other domestic activities. 

E-12. Spectrum management supporting domestic operations requires liaison with federal, state, municipal, local, and tribal authorities and first responders. Spectrum management for operations within the continental United States must comply with a complex legal, regulatory, and policy framework. USNORTHCOM and USINDOPACOM are the principal planning agents for defense support of civil authorities. They provide joint planning and execution directives for peacetime assistance rendered by the DOD within their areas of responsibility. The other combatant commands provide capabilities to USNORTHCOM and USINDOPACOM for defense support of civil authorities as directed by the Secretary of Defense. 

E-13. Each state’s separate joint force headquarters retains responsibility for forces operating within that state or territory. The Army National Guard G-6 spectrum management branch coordinates and plans spectrum management for Army National Guard forces. It provides support to the state’s spectrum managers for domestic operations. The National Guard G-6 spectrum management branch includes coordination

Spectrum managers from joint task force headquarters form a JEMSOC supporting operational task forces under state active duty or dual status (Title 32 and Title 10) domestic operations. 

E-14. After acquiring frequency assignments, activated forces may operate civil and military systems within an assigned area to enhance interoperability with other federal agencies and civil authorities. National Guard forces may request frequency assignments through either National Guard or Title 10 military channels, depending on their duty status for a given operation. Like numerous states, local agencies, and federal agencies, National Guard forces and the Coast Guard may operate civil and military systems within a domestic area. 

E-15. The regional spectrum manager or the JEMSOC submits a standard frequency action format request for frequencies on behalf of end users. A state spectrum manager provides spectrum management functions for a given state to the greatest extent possible. Spectrum managers coordinate regional operations and spectrum deconfliction for operations adjacent to another spectrum manager’s assigned area. Local spectrum managers form the JEMSOC and work directly for the incident commander or the state joint force headquarters before the appointment of an incident commander. 

E-16. Lessons learned from past domestic operations have demonstrated that unity of effort and coordinated spectrum management are critical to the operation’s success. Congress and the DOD, through the implementation of a dual-status commander (commander of both Title 32 National Guard forces and Title 10 National Guard of the United States and Active Component forces for a domestic operation), have addressed unity of effort. Unity of command is not applicable between federal military forces and the state National Guard. Still, unity of effort is possible if the President and the governor formally agree to appoint a dualstatus commander. Established federal authorities allow a designated dual-status commander to serve in a hybrid federal and state status. A dual-status commander is usually a National Guard officer who exercises simultaneous but separate authority over federal and state military forces. Spectrum management for a domestic operation requires consolidation under one JEMSOC or a coordinated Title 10 and Title 32 JEMSOC led by the same commander to minimize confusion and provide seamless support to tactical communications. 

E-17. The JEMSOC initially assigned to an incident continues to work for the incident commander as the operation transitions to a different duty status or legal authority to ensure continuity of spectrum management. Typically, a state governor or adjutant general creates a standing joint task force, including a JEMSOC, or establishes a joint task force with a JEMSOC in response to an incident. If a state does not have qualified spectrum managers, the Adjutant General and governor may request spectrum managers deploy from other states, forming a JEMSOC under formalized emergency management assistance compact. ##### **SECTION II – TERMS** 

###### **common operational picture** 

A single, identical display of relevant information shared by more than one command that facilitates collaborative planning and assists all echelons to achieve situational awareness. (JP 3-0) 

###### **cyberspace domain** 

(Army) The interdependent networks of information technology infrastructures and resident data, including the Internet, telecommunication networks, computer systems, embedded processors and controllers, and relevant portions of the electromagnetic spectrum. (FM 3-0) 

###### **electromagnetic environmental effects** 

The impact of the electromagnetic environment upon the operational capability of military forces, equipment, systems, and platforms. (JP 3-85) 

###### **electromagnetic interference** 

Any electromagnetic disturbance, induced intentionally or unintentionally, that interrupts, obstructs, or otherwise degrades or limits the effective performance of electromagnetic spectrum-dependent systems and electrical equipment. (JP 3-85) 

###### **electromagnetic operational environment** 

Is a composite of the actual and potential electromagnetic energy radiation, conditions, circumstances, and influences that affect the employment of capabilities and the decisions of the commander. (JP 3-85) 

###### **electromagnetic pulse** 

A strong burst of electromagnetic radiation caused by a nuclear explosion, energy weapon, or natural phenomenon that may couple with electrical or electronic systems to produce damaging current and voltage surges. (JP 3-85) 

###### **electromagnetic radiation hazards** 

Transmitter or antenna installation that generates or increases electromagnetic radiation in the vicinity of ordnance, personnel, or fueling operations in excess of established safe levels. (JP 3-85)

**Glossary** 

###### **frequency deconfliction** 

A systematic management procedure to coordinate the use of the electromagnetic spectrum for operations, communications, and intelligence functions. (JP 3-85) 

###### **guarded frequencies** 

A list of time-oriented, enemy frequencies that are currently being exploited for combat information and intelligence or jammed after the commander has weighed the potential operational gain against the loss of the technical information. (JP 3-85) 

###### **joint restricted frequency list** 

A time and geographically oriented listing of TABOO, protected, and guarded functions, nets, and frequencies and limited to the minimum number of frequencies necessary for friendly forces to accomplish objectives. (JP 3-85) 

###### **military decision-making process** 

An iterative planning methodology to understand the situation and mission, develop a course of action, and produce an operation plan or order. (ADP 5-0) 

###### **protected frequencies** 

Friendly, generally time-oriented, frequencies used for a particular operation, identified and protected to prevent them from being inadvertently jammed by friendly forces while active electromagnetic warfare operations are directed against hostile forces. (JP 3-85) 

###### **spectrum management operations** 

The interrelated functions of spectrum management, frequency assignment, host-nation coordination, and policy that enable the planning, management, and execution of operations within the electromagnetic operational environment during all phases of military operations. (FM 6-02) 

###### **TABOO frequencies** 

Any friendly frequency of such importance that it must never be deliberately jammed or interfered with by friendly forces including international distress, safety, and controller frequencies. (JP 3-85) 

###### **unified action partners** 

Those military forces, governmental and nongovernmental organizations, and elements of the private sector with whom Army forces plan, coordinate, synchronize, and integrate during the conduct of operations. (FM 3-0)
