### **Introduction** 

GEOINT, in its most basic form as an intelligence discipline, includes— 

- Imagery. 

- Imagery intelligence. 

- Geospatial information. 

This composition forms the foundation of Army GEOINT products and allows the Army to build on that foundation by fusing and layering various intelligence and command and control information with a geospatial component. ATP 2-22.7 comprises seven chapters and five appendixes: 

- **Chapter 1** summarizes GEOINT, including imagery, imagery intelligence, and geospatial information. It also provides an overview of the National System for Geospatial Intelligence (NSG) and the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency. 

- **Chapter 2** discusses GEOINT roles and responsibilities—from those organizations and programs supporting the NSG to the Army Geospatial Enterprise, through which the Army integrates with other NSG members to improve the quality of intelligence support to operations. 

- **Chapter 3** discusses GEOINT in the plan and direct step of intelligence process. 

- **Chapter 4** discusses GEOINT in the collect and process step of the intelligence process. 

- **Chapter 5** discusses GEOINT production and its dissemination and integration. 

- **Chapter 6** describes GEOINT activities across the Army strategic contexts. 

- **Chapter 7** provides considerations for joint multinational operations and unique environments. 

- **Appendix A** details imagery types and characteristics. 

- **Appendix B** provides policy considerations and domestic imagery requirements. 

- **Appendix C** details GEOINT collection assets and systems. 

- **Appendix D** discusses GEOINT support to targeting, including the decide, detect, deliver, and assess functions of the Army targeting process. 

- **Appendix E** discusses the GEOINT integrated architecture, including Army GEOINT architecture, processing systems, and software and application tools. 

This publication— 

- Uses the term _threat_ , which includes all enemies and adversaries that are part of the operational environment. 

- Uses the word _theater_ to indicate theater of operations. 

- Uses the generic term _intelligence analysis system_ due to the variety of fielded and partially fielded systems and applications. 

- Introduces acronyms at their first use in the front matter of this publication (preface and introduction), and again in the body of the publication (chapters and appendixes). 

- Introduces the _G-X_ , _S-X_ , and _J-X_ (such as _G-2_ , _S-3_ , _J-2_ ) acronyms at their first use without defining them as it hinders readability. Definitions for these acronyms can be found in the glossary of this publication. 

- Uses _G-2/S-2_ , _G-3/S-3_ , and _G-6/S-6_ to denote division or corps/battalion or brigade intelligence, operations, and signal staff officers, respectively. 

- Uses _U.S._ as a modifier (for example, _U.S. forces_ ) and _United States_ as a noun (for example, the _United States, a country in North America_ ).

### **Geospatial Intelligence (GEOINT) Overview** 

#### **WHAT IS GEOINT?** 

1-1. _Geospatial intelligence_ is the exploitation and analysis of imagery and geospatial information to describe, assess, and visually depict physical features and geographically referenced activities on or about the Earth. Geospatial intelligence consists of imagery, imagery intelligence, and geospatial information (JP 2-0). 

1-2. GEOINT is one of the single-source intelligence disciplines nested within the intelligence process (plan and direct, collect and process, produce, and disseminate and integrate), which generates information, products, and knowledge about threats, terrain and weather, and civil considerations. This supports the conduct (plan, prepare, execute, and assess) of operations across the Army strategic contexts (competition below armed conflict, crisis, and armed conflict). GEOINT provides geospatial information and intelligence that supports the commander and staff and the development of all-source intelligence. (FM 2-0 provides doctrine on the intelligence disciplines and the intelligence process; FM 3-0 provides doctrine on the Army strategic contexts.) 

1-3. GEOINT provides supported commanders with information that provides situational awareness and facilitates the military decision-making process (MDMP). GEOINT operations and information are also instrumental in corroborating other intelligence discipline reporting and cueing other discipline operations. 

##### **IMAGERY** 

1-4. _Imagery_ is a likeness or presentation of any natural or man-made feature or related object or activity, and the positional data acquired at the same time the likeness or representation was acquired, including: products produced by space-based national intelligence reconnaissance systems; and likeness and presentations produced by satellites, airborne platforms, unmanned aerial vehicles, or other similar means (except that such term does not include handheld or clandestine photography taken by or on behalf of human intelligence collection organizations) (JP 2-0). Imagery is the visual basis of GEOINT, enabling the intelligence staff to see the operational environment (OE) without physically being there. (See appendix A for imagery types.) 

##### **IMAGERY INTELLIGENCE** 

1-5. _Imagery intelligence_ is the technical, geographic, and intelligence information derived through the interpretation or analysis of imagery and collateral materials (JP 2-0). Imagery intelligence (IMINT) provides concrete, detailed, and precise image-based information on the location and activity of a threat and the environment's physical characteristics. It detects key natural and terrain features, installations, and infrastructure—information that is used to develop intelligence studies, reports, and target folders. 

1-6. IMINT assists commanders in applying and protecting their combat power and allows commanders to see the area of operations (AO) in near real time as the operation progresses. IMINT supports decision making and facilitates situational understanding by reducing uncertainty about the threat and OE. It also facilitates the intelligence preparation of the operational environment (IPOE) process, is included in all-source analysis, and is used to develop analytical and IPOE products.

##### **GEOSPATIAL INFORMATION** 

1-7. Joint doctrine defines _geospatial information and services_ as the collection, information extraction, storage, dissemination, and exploitation of geodetic, geomagnetic, imagery (both commercial and national source), gravimetric, aeronautical, topographic, hydrographic, littoral, cultural, and toponymic data accurately referenced to a precise location on the surface of the Earth (JP 2-0). 

1-8. _Geospatial information_ is information that identifies the geographic location and characteristics of natural or constructed features and boundaries on or about the Earth, including: data and information derived from, among other things, remote sensing, mapping, and surveying technologies; and mapping, charting, geomatics data, and related products and services (JP 2-0). 

#### **THE NATIONAL SYSTEM FOR GEOSPATIAL INTELLIGENCE** 

1-9. The _National System for Geospatial Intelligence_ is the combination of technology, policies, capabilities, doctrine, activities, people, data, and organizations necessary to produce geospatial intelligence in an integrated, multi-intelligence environment (JP 2-0). The mission of the National System for Geospatial Intelligence (NSG) is to create an integrated, collaborative community of common practice, promoting GEOINT across a diverse group of producers and consumers. 

1-10. The functional manager for GEOINT, serving concurrently as the Defense Intelligence Enterprise Manager for GEOINT, manages the NSG. The NSG manages operations through guidance, policy, programs, and organizations. (See appendix B for policy considerations.) It fosters collaboration among U.S. Government departments and agencies, the Services, echelons, and multinational partners to provide decision makers, commanders, intelligence users and producers, and civil authorities a better understanding of GEOINT. 

1-11. During information collection planning, intelligence staffs leverage support from each of these elements. For example, when intelligence staffs develop intelligence communications networks, the NSG can assist them in identifying those organizations or agencies that should be included in the command’s intelligence architecture. The National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA), under the guidance of the NGA director and GEOINT Enterprise management through the National GEOINT Committee, can advise on integrating NSG members and partners (see table 1-1) on information collection strategies. This collaboration leverages GEOINT for the Army, resulting in more comprehensive and tailored intelligence products across the warfighting functions. 

1-12. The NSG, in providing GEOINT to the Nation’s decision makers and Soldiers, depends on its close collaboration with multinational partners. The NGA director, serving as the U.S. GEOINT representative to the Allied System for Geospatial Intelligence (ASG) Senior GEOINT Council, may advise NGA on the input from multinational partners, through the ASG, to provide integrated, unique, and assured GEOINT to the Nation’s warfighters and policy makers to inform decisions and enable action. Additionally, as a member of the U.S. intelligence community (IC), NGA executes the tasking of imagery collection, coordination of imagery processing, and exploitation activities, ensuring the timely dissemination of imagery to authorized recipients. 

1-13. Commanders generally employ organic GEOINT resources against time-sensitive and critical information needs. They rely on nonorganic resources to provide medium to long-term collection and analysis. Intelligence staffs assist commanders in understanding how to use nonorganic GEOINT resources that contain or collect data related to a theater. 

1-14. The NSG comprises members from U.S. IC and DOD organizations under GEOINT functional management authorities, as outlined in Executive Order 12333 as amended, DODD 5105.60, and Intelligence Community Directive 113. (See table 1-1.) NSG members prioritize, plan, program, budget, acquire, collect, analyze, produce, share, store, and process GEOINT across national, military, and civil domains.

#### **NATIONAL GEOSPATIAL-INTELLIGENCE AGENCY** 

1-15. NGA, established in Section 441, Title 10, United States Code, is a DOD combat support agency that collects, analyzes, and disseminates GEOINT data and products. NGA manages satellite collection requirements and develops distribution protocols for the NSG according to the National Intelligence Priorities Framework (also known as NIPF), which is managed by the Director of National Intelligence (DNI). 

1-16. The NGA director serves as the functional manager for GEOINT in accordance with applicable laws and guidance from the DNI as well as DOD directives and agreements. The NGA director— 

- Serves as principal adviser to the DNI for performance of the GEOINT function. 

- Exercises functional management and oversight of the NSG, including technical oversight of NSG tactical elements, to ensure interoperability between existing and future NSG systems, connectivity between national and tactical systems, and modernization of tactical systems. 

- Manages GEOINT activities. 

- Sets standards for the GEOINT architecture and products. **_Note._** A reachback component at NGA Headquarters focuses on NGA production support. Commanders and staffs can access NGA products directly from the NGA website on NIPRNET. 

- Provides technical guidance for systems using GEOINT. 

##### **NATIONAL GEOSPATIAL-INTELLIGENCE AGENCY SUPPORT TEAM** 

1-17. NGA directly supports joint force intelligence operations centers through its National GeospatialIntelligence Agency support teams (NSTs) and maintains these teams for DOD agencies, several non-DOD agencies, and each of the Services. 

1-18. An NST is the primary mechanism of interaction between NGA, federal departments, the Services, combatant commands (CCMDs), and other organizations. NSTs provide timely, accurate, and tailored GEOINT to CCMDs during peacetime, throughout exercises, and during war. They act as a conduit for ensuring the fusion of available information between NGA and CCMDs.

   - Army’s ability to protect and secure its forces, information, technologies, and other resources.

##### **U.S. ARMY SPECIAL OPERATIONS COMMAND** 

2-5. U.S. Army Special Operations Command special forces groups and its 75th Ranger Regiment have both GEOINT imagery analysts and geospatial engineers. Civil affairs brigades have geospatial engineers, whereas the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment only has GEOINT imagery analysts. An NGA GEOINT imagery analyst also supports each of these organizations; NGA experts assist in interpreting and exploiting GEOINT products and services. 

2-6. U.S. Army Special Operations Command units may collect full-motion video (FMV) using available or theater GEOINT collection assets tasked to support the unit. The unit’s task-organized GEOINT cell uses this FMV along with existing intelligence to conduct geospatial analysis and develop GEOINT products. 

**_Note._** _Available collection assets_ describe organic, assigned, and attached assets as well as those assets under operational control or in direct support of that unit. The unit lists organic, assigned, attached, operational control, and supporting units and the overall task organization in Annex A (Task Organization) to the order. 

##### **ARMY GEOINT OFFICE** 

2-7. The Army GEOINT Office is the onsite representative at NGA Headquarters and the formal Army point of contact for NGA. The office integrates with the Army Deputy Chief of Staff for Intelligence (DA G-2), FORSCOM, INSCOM, the U.S. Army Intelligence Center of Excellence, and all relevant Army GEOINT stakeholders to produce fully coordinated Army-NSG positions, ensure active participation in NSG governance forums, and perform integrated mission management. It also supports the planning, programming, budgeting, acquisition, technology investment, policy, doctrine, training, tradecraft, personnel, tasking, collection, production, and processing, exploitation, and dissemination (PED) functions related to military GEOINT functions and activities. 

##### **ARMY NATIONAL TO THEATER PROGRAM** 

2-8. The Army National to Theater Program serves as the NSG’s primary producer of GEOINT supporting theater information requirements for CCMDs. The program contains the Army’s portion of GEOINT support to the U.S. IC as designed by the NGA director. The Army National to Theater Program, in conjunction with NSG member organizations, executes unified GEOINT operations across the U.S. IC and CCMDs. 

2-9. Functional management authority for the Army National to Theater Program resides with the DA G-2 or its appointed agent. The operations manager, located at Headquarters, INSCOM G-3, implements instructions from the program functional manager and assists with program execution reviews, as required. 

2-10. Five integrated GEOINT divisions (also known as IGDs) execute the Army National to Theater Program. These divisions reside within the six INSCOM military intelligence brigades-theater (MIB-Ts)— 66th, 207th, 470th, 500th, 501st, and 513th—and geographically align with Army Service component commands (ASCCs). They respond to intelligence requirements by converting national and theater GEOINT collection into intelligence products using various GEOINT phenomenologies. 

###### **National Ground Intelligence Center Support** 

2-11. NGIC Applied Remote Sensing (formerly known as the Ground Spectral Intelligence Center) is a specialized subordinate organization of the Army National to Theater Program. It is the Army’s center for image science, including spectral and overhead persistent infrared (OPIR) expertise; it conducts OPIR research and development. The AGB OPIR PED capability also resides within NGIC. 

###### **U.S. Army Space and Missile Defense Command** 

2-12. The U.S. Army Space and Missile Defense Command (also known as USASMDC), located at Petersen Space Force Base, is also an Army National to Theater Program specialized subordinate organization. It provides a range of national satellite systems and commercial GEOINT services and products to designated civil agencies, other DOD forces, U.S. Space Command, U.S. Northern Command, U.S. Strategic Command, and their subordinate joint forces worldwide.

**GEOINT Roles and Responsibilities** 

2-13. The U.S. Army Space and Missile Defense Command GEOINT Division is a GEOINT unit specializing in advanced production using nonliteral analysis of imagery data obtained from visible and invisible portions of the electromagnetic spectrum (EMS). (See appendix A.) The GEOINT Division— 

- Creates detailed geospatial information and services (GI&S) and mapping data. 

- Conducts analysis of airborne and satellite imagery, including synthetic aperture radar (SAR), panchromatic, multispectral, hyperspectral, thermal, FMV, OPIR, and light detection and ranging (LIDAR) imagery. 

- Fields requests for support and access to its products via the Geospatial Intelligence Enterprise Tasking, Processing, Exploitation, and Dissemination Service (GETS). (See paragraph 5-23.) 

##### **ARMY GEOSPATIAL CENTER**

##### **Supporting the COP** 

<mark>A</mark> _<mark>common operational picture</mark>_ <mark>is a single, identical display of relevant information shared by more than one command that facilitates collaborative planning and assists all echelons to achieve situational awareness (JP 3-0). Geospatial information and imagery provide the foundation for the COP, which facilitates situational understanding for commanders and the warfighting functions. The COP is critical in integrating Army operations because it provides a common view of operations, established command standards, and aspects of the OE.</mark> 

<mark>Controlled image base (CIB) is a visual planning tool that supports the COP, mission planning, and C2 systems. CIB is a standard NGA dataset of panchromatic orthorectified (see paragraph 4-74) digital imagery from national satellite systems or other adequate commercial imagery. CIB also supports weapon systems, regional overviews, and mapping where maps are nonexistent or outdated.</mark> 

<mark>CIB imagery is useful for image map backgrounds and displaying features not represented on digital map backgrounds. CIB can be used for urban areas, but it may not provide the detailed resolution needed for urban analysis. In this situation, a higher resolution from commercial or national sources is better suited. CIB conforms to both the raster product format and National Imagery Transmission Format standard, and it uses World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS 84) datum (see paragraph 4-71). ##### **STEP 3: COURSE OF ACTION DEVELOPMENT** 

3-8. The staff develops options for subsequent analysis and comparison to satisfy the commander’s intent and planning guidance. ##### **STEP 4: COURSE OF ACTION ANALYSIS** 

3-10. COA analysis begins once the commander selects one or more acceptable COAs from the COA development step. As part of COA analysis, the staff prepares to war-game the COAs. 

###### **Role-Playing the Enemy** 

3-11. During the war game, the G-2/S-2 role-plays the enemy commander, other threat organizations, and critical civil considerations in the AO. The G-2/S-2 captures the results of each enemy, threat group, and civil consideration action and counteraction as well as the corresponding friendly and enemy strengths and vulnerabilities. By trying to win the war game for the enemy, the G-2/S-2 ensures the staff addresses friendly responses for each enemy COA. This enables the G-2/S-2 to refine friendly intelligence requirements, the event template and corresponding decision points, target areas of interest, and high-value targets (HVTs). 

###### **Reacting to Enemy Courses of Action** 

3-12. As new potential enemy reactions to friendly COAs are considered during the war game, the GEOINT cell assists in filling correlated intelligence gaps with current imagery and geospatial data. The cell supports the war-gaming effort by maintaining current products that depict enemy locations and movements, refining operations graphics, coordinating with the fusion cell to refine and revise products to the intelligence staff’s needs, and responding to new RFIs as they arise. During war gaming, the GEOINT cell— 

- Creates data from enemy locations and movements. 

- Updates friendly and enemy force unit positions. 

- Coordinates with outside agencies, other units, and the fusion cell to refine products.

###### **_Observation and Fields of Fire_** 

3-27. _Observation_ is the condition of weather and terrain that permits a force to see the friendly, enemy, and neutral personnel and systems, and key aspects of the environment (FM 1-02.1). Examples include but are not limited to fortified locations on higher elevation with a cleared field of fire or lookout towers. Some variables to consider when obtaining data related to observation are topography, vegetation, urban development, and man-made obscurants such as smoke or defensive perimeters. 

3-28. A _field of fire_ is the area that a weapon or group of weapons may cover effectively from a given position (FM 3-90). Observation directly relates to a unit’s field of fire. One example is open land with a clear view within available weaponry’s range. Observation determines the field of fire for direct fire weapons and lines of fire determine the field of fire for indirect capabilities. The GEOINT cell identifies observation and fields of fire by— 

- Supporting dynamic LOS analysis by obtaining detailed elevation data through research and intelligence reach databases. 

- Obtaining available terrain categorization data.

###### **_Avenues of Approach_** 

3-32. An a _venue of approach_ is a path used by an attacking force leading to its objective or to key terrain. Avenues of approach exist in all domains (ADP 3-90). The breadth and difficulty of an attacking unit’s size limits its avenue of approach (AA). Variables that can affect an AA include surface conditions, topography, and drainage characteristics. AAs include but are not limited to LOCs, such as roads, rail lines, rivers, and areas where movement of troops, weapons, and equipment could occur. AAs also include mobility corridors (areas that are relatively free of obstacles where a force will be canalized due to terrain restrictions allowing military forces to capitalize on the principles of mass and speed). To identify AAs, the GEOINT cell researches and obtains GEOINT data on current LOCs through the OE, catalogs mobility corridors within the OE, and categorizes AAs supporting either threat or friendly capabilities. 

###### **_Key Terrain_** 

3-33. _Key terrain_ is an identifiable characteristic whose seizure or retention affords a marked advantage to either combatant (ADP 3-90). Two factors can render an area key terrain: how a commander wants to use the area and whether the enemy can use it to defeat friendly forces. Key terrain typically offers control of a local objective or an important transportation route. The GEOINT cell determines key terrain by identifying— 

- High ground with good observation and fields of fire. 

- Transportation chokepoints such as a water crossing, mountain gap, or road junction. 

- Dense woods or rivers that anchor a battle line’s flank. 

3-34. _Decisive terrain_ is key terrain whose seizure and retention is mandatory for successful mission accomplishment (ADP 3-90). Also referred to as critical terrain, decisive terrain is relatively rare and not present in every battle. The GEOINT cell determines decisive terrain by identifying— 

- Urban areas. 

- LOCs and supply routes. 

- Topography. 

- Bridges and chokepoints. 

- Key military installations.

###### **_Obstacles_** 

3-35. An _obstacle_ is any barrier designed or employed to disrupt, fix, turn, or block the movement and maneuver, and to impose additional losses in personnel, time, and equipment (JP 3-15). The presence and relative difficulty of obstacles determine whether terrain is unrestricted, restricted, or severely restricted. Analysts categorize the hindrance level of obstacles as _go_ , _slow go_ , or _no go_ . The GEOINT cell identifies obstacles and determines their effects on the OE by analyzing existing obstacle layers. They identify additional obstacles in the OE by analyzing new GEOINT data. 

3-36. Obstacles include but are not limited to the following: 

- Vegetation (swamps, dense forests, open fields, and farmland). 

- Topography. 

- Fences. 

- Bridges. 

- Reinforcing and fortification features (parapets, stone walls, and vehicle barriers). 

- Ditches. 

- Battle events. 

- Urban areas. 

- Water (streams, rivers, lakes, and irrigation canals). 

- Drainage characteristics (natural and man-made). 

- Wet and dry surface materials (ravines and bluffs). 

**_Note._** Friendly or threat forces place reinforcing obstacles within the OE to slow, stop, or control opposition movement. Examples include but are not limited to barriers and revetments, blown bridges, and cratered runways. 

###### **_Cover and Concealment_** 

3-37. _Cover_ is protection from the effects of fires (FM 3-96). Examples include ditches, riverbanks, buildings and structures, walls, and entrenchments. _Concealment_ is protection from observation or surveillance (FM 3-96). Examples include forests, ravines, dense vegetation, and reverse slopes. To determine cover and concealment, the GEOINT cell identifies terrain that offers protection from the effects of fire and from observation or surveillance.

These conditions can enhance or limit operations by impacting trafficability, water crossing (fording), and visibility.

###### **_Counter Threat Activity_** 

3-58. The GEOINT cell leverages multiple sensors to counter explosive hazards threats such as mine manufacturing and emplacement. GEOINT imagery analysts use multispectral imagery to analyze NAIs likely associated with homemade explosive manufacturing signatures. GEOINT imagery analysts use thermal imaging to detect cooling and heating signatures. Thermal imaging can detect signatures of disturbed earth or seek buried objects that cool or heat at a different rate from the surrounding soil. The cell links suspected cache areas to support areas using imagery, change detection, and MTI. GEOINT takes multiple products relating to points of interest and links them to refine areas requiring further attention from collection or operations.

##### **SECTION I – COLLECTION** 

4-1. _Collection_ is, in intelligence usage, the acquisition of information and the provision of this information to processing elements (JP 2-0). The commander focuses collection by establishing priorities, asking questions of intelligence relevance, and approving PIRs recommended by the staff during the MDMP. The commander’s information needs and intelligence gaps drive GEOINT collection operations. GEOINT requirements necessitate tasking and collecting imagery, geospatial data, and completed products. GEOINT collection operations use satellites, manned and unmanned aircraft, and reach to collect data from existing requirements and derive new requirements. 

#### **GEOINT COLLECTION** 

4-2. GEOINT collection entails developing RFCs; validating, prioritizing, and tasking collection requirements; assessing the collection; and providing feedback. The collection management team performs this process to support the commander’s intelligence and operational priorities. The team prioritizes collection requirements and plans, schedules, and tasks GEOINT collection assets. GEOINT collection results in either the direct tasking of GEOINT collection assets, over which the supported commander has authority, or the generation of an RFC that can be supported at a higher, lower, or adjacent echelon. 

4-3. In joint doctrine, collection management has two subordinate functions anchored under collection management authority: 

- _Collection requirements management_ is the authoritative development and control of collection, processing, exploitation, and/or reporting requirements that normally result in the direct tasking of requirements to units over which the commander has authority, or the generation of tasking requests to collection management authorities at a higher lower, or lateral echelon to accomplish the collection mission (JP 2-0). 

- _Collection operations management_ is the authoritative direction, scheduling, and control of specific collection operations and associated processing, exploitation, and information reporting resources (JP 2-0). 

4-4. In Army doctrine, collection management has three functions that are based on a tactical level point of view (see ATP 2-01): 

- **Requirements management** identifies what requirements to collect against and when to collect. This function is performed through collaboration by the collection management team, commander, requesters, and the rest of the staff. 

- **Mission management** specifies what assets should collect against intelligence requirements and defines when, where, and how to employ those assets and PED capabilities. This function is performed through collaboration by the collection management team, operations staff, and collection assets or the assets’ C2 element. 

- **Execution management** ensures the continuous integration and synchronization of ongoing information collection. This function is performed through collaboration by commanders and staffs that control current operations with support from the collection management team. 

##### **DEVELOP THE REQUEST FOR COLLECTION** 

4-5. RFCs differ from RFIs. RFIs require researching and analyzing existing data to answer intelligence problem sets; RFCs drive collection assets to generate new collection. RFCs must be submitted through the collection management team to be validated, prioritized, and processed for action. The submission process begins when the requester generates an RFC and ends when the collection requirement is satisfied.

###### **Define Collection Parameters** 

4-9. RFC parameters determine how a sensor will be used. The RFC articulates where the GEOINT collection asset will go, when it will be there, and what will happen once it arrives over the objective.

4-10. The location of the objective or target is the most important information when developing an RFC. The requester cannot research or direct the sensor to the point of collection without grid coordinates. The target or point of collection for theater and below sensors is identified using the military grid reference system (MGRS). (See paragraph 4-69.) The target or point of collection for national level sensors is identified using the national technical means latitude and longitude. 

4-11. When submitting requests for new GEOINT collection, a requester can select from five target types to determine the target location and size. The requester chooses the target type based on size, desired image type, and priority and timeline for collection. The five types of targets are— 

- Point. 

- LOC. 

- Directed search area (also known as DSA). 

- Broad area search (also known as BAS). 

- Global geospatial information services (also known as GGIS), formerly known as mapping, charting, and geodesy.

4-12. The activity or intelligence the requester is attempting to gather determines when the sensor collects it. Some RFCs may require a specific collection time frame based on operational needs, while others simply require collection within a 24-hour period with no time requirements.

###### **_Periodicity_** 

4-13. In addition to time requirements, an RFC must specify collection periodicity (how often or how many times the sensor must collect within a given timeframe). RFCs express periodicity in different ways: 

- Two numbers separated by a colon convey reconnaissance-focused collection periodicity (1:1, 1:3, 2:1, 1:7). The first number represents the number of collections, while the second number represents collection frequency. For example, 1:1 indicates a request for one collection every day, 1:3 indicates one collection every 3 days, and 2:1 represents two collections every day. 

- A block of time in Zulu format (such as 0200Z–0500Z) conveys surveillance-focused collection periodicity. The time block should reflect the critical hours requested per day. For example, a request can specify a 4-hour time block in the morning and a 6-hour time block in the evening. 

###### **_Sensor Actions_** 

4-14. The RFC should also define what the sensor is going to do when it arrives on the target or point of collection. This information tells the sensor operator what the requester expects from the collection and provides parameters for building a collection strategy. For example, a surveillance focused RFC identifies whether the sensor is required to loiter over the same target for the duration of the collection period or switch between multiple objectives. 

###### **Justify the Collection Requirement** 

4-15. The collection requirement justification explains why the unit needs the collection and how the intelligence gained will aid in filling identified intelligence gaps in the supported commander’s PIRs. For example, an RFC to support operations must explain what the operation is, why the collection is needed, and how it will support the operation. The collection management team uses the justification to support the request prioritization. Justification for collection support should consider— 

- **Specific operation.** What operation will the sensor support? 

- **Target development.** What is the specific target? 

- **Intelligence requirement.** What PIR is the request attempting to answer? 

- **Force protection.** What is the force protection condition associated with the request?

- The appropriate organization to perform the exploitation. 

- The organization that will perform the exploitation. 

- The essential elements of information (also called EEIs) to be exploited, based on collaboration with the analyst. (In other words, what the analyst wants.)

##### **GEOREFERENCING** 

4-72. _Georeferencing_ refers to the process of aligning geographic data to a known coordinate system so it can be viewed, queried, and analyzed with other geographic data. Georeferencing may involve shifting, rotating, scaling, skewing, and, in some situations, warping, rubber sheeting, or orthorectifying the data. Georeferencing is the recording of the absolute location of data points. Georeferencing is a requisite task for all spatial data, since spatial data cannot be positioned in space or evaluated regarding other data without being assigned a spatial coordinate within a defined coordinate system. 

**_Note._** _Rubber sheeting_ refers to a procedure for adjusting the coordinates of all the points in a dataset to allow a more accurate match between known locations and a few points within the set. Rubber sheeting preserves the interconnectivity between points and objects through stretching, shrinking, or reorienting their interconnecting lines. 

##### **RECTIFICATION** 

4-73. Orthorectification and georectification are rectification methods that vary due to the different techniques, algorithms, and software used. 

###### **Orthorectification** 

4-74. _Orthorectification_ refers to a process of correcting imagery for geometric distortions caused by errors related to topography, atmosphere, maps, measuring instruments, GEOINT collection assets, or sensors. It is an accurate method of correcting an image to align with real-world coordinates, creating a true map. 

4-75. Orthorectification involves precisely measuring the photo center’s exact location and the exact camera angle when the image is acquired. The process requires camera calibration parameters to remove cameraand lens-included distortions, and a digital elevation model of the earth to remove terrain-included distortions. Orthorectification results in very high positional accuracy or uniform scale throughout the image. For example, after orthorectification, it becomes appropriate to measure or precisely locate features in the image, to collect information for a geographic information system (also called GIS), or to combine the image with other accurately rectified images for analyses.

###### **Georectification** 

4-76. _Georectification_ (not to be confused with georeferencing) refers to a method of stretching and warping an image to align with a map or other spatial data in a geographic information system, thus removing geometric distortions between sets of data points. The process matches image data to a set of geographical coordinates so that each pixel of the image is assigned a geographical coordinate. 

4-77. Georectification does not require the location of points with space; only adjustment of the relative position of those points to one another. Several corresponding control points, such as street intersections, are marked on both the image and the map. These locations become reference points in the subsequent processing of the image. On the image, _x, y_ coordinates are determined for each corresponding ground control point. The relationship of the _x, y_ image coordinates to real-world ground control points is used to determine the algorithm to warp the image to the map. The resulting accuracy can never exceed the accuracy of the reference data (a 1:50,000 topographic line map is only accurate to within 66 feet or 23 meters). This method may be acceptable for projects with low accuracy requirements.

### **GEOINT Across the Army Strategic Contexts** 

#### **COMPETITION BELOW ARMED CONFLICT** 

6-1. Competition below armed conflict exists when two or more state or nonstate adversaries have incompatible interests, but neither seeks armed conflict. Nation-states compete using the instruments of national power to gain and maintain advantages to achieve their goals. Low levels of lethal force can be part of competition. Adversaries often employ cyberspace capabilities and information warfare to destroy or disrupt infrastructure, interfere with democratic processes, and conduct other malign activities in a way that does not cause the United States and its allies to respond with force. Competition also includes cooperation with allies and partners to counter pacing threats. 

6-2. During competition, GEOINT focuses on training, target development, and support to IPOE. All activities should support the transition to armed conflict. ##### **Appendix A** 

### **Imagery Types and Characteristics** 

#### **OVERVIEW** 

A-1. GEOINT collection assets gather data across the EMS, using a variety of sensors to produce different types of imagery. The characteristics of each imagery type determine how it is used. 

**_Note._** This appendix uses terms from NSG’s _Geospatial Intelligence (GEOINT) Basic Doctrine, Publication 1.0_ (hereinafter referred to as Pub 1.0) to describe imagery sensors and types. The terms do not appear in the Glossary. 

#### **IMAGERY ALONG THE ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM** 

A-2. The EMS is the entire range of electromagnetic radiation frequencies. It is a maneuver space essential for facilitating control within the OE, affecting all portions of the OE and military operations. Military operations and training are executed in an environment complicated by increasingly challenging demands and constraints on the EMS. Just as in the physical domains and in cyberspace, military forces maneuver and conduct operations within the EMS to achieve tactical, operational, and strategic advantages. Freedom of maneuver and action within the EMS are essential to U.S. and multinational operations. (For doctrine on the EMS, see JP 3-85.) 

A-3. The EMS comprises many subranges (referred to as portions) that are named based on the behavior of waves’ emission, transmission, and absorption and on their applications. Boundaries between contiguous portions of the EMS are undefined, so the ranges overlap. The EMS—from the lowest frequency to the highest (longest to shortest wavelength)—includes radio waves, infrared radiation, visible light, ultraviolet radiation, x rays, and gamma rays. 

A-4. GEOINT systems generally collect data in the following EMS ranges (exact values depend on the sensor) (see figure A-1): 

- **Visible light** (400–700 nanometers) is the visible portion of the EMS. 

- **Near infrared (also known as NIR)** (700–1,100 nanometers) is used primarily for imaging vegetation and detecting camouflage. 

- **Short-wave infrared (also known as SWIR)** (1,100–3,000 nanometers) is used for imaging vegetation, soil moisture content, forest fires, geological features, silicates, and clays. 

- **Mid-wave infrared (also known as MWIR)** (3,000–5,000 nanometers) is used for night studies, imaging geological structures, and detecting thermal differences in water currents, fires, and effluents (uses emitted radiation and some reflected radiation during daytime imaging). 

- **Long-wave infrared (also known as LWIR)** (7,000–14,000 nanometers) is used for environmental studies and for imaging subtle differences in temperature between objects, gas plumes, and effluents using emitted energy. 

- **Microwave** (1 millimeter–1 meter) is used for radar, SAR, and related technologies; useful for mapping terrain, day and night all-weather imaging, detecting objects under canopy, and communication paths.

#### **IMAGERY SENSORS** 

A-5. All forms of imaging occur through interaction between a sensor and the object being observed. Some GEOINT collection assets can be configured to carry a variety of sensor packages. Each sensor and GEOINT collection asset have a unique capability with distinct advantages and disadvantages. There are two types of imagery sensors: active and passive. 

##### **ACTIVE SENSORS** 

A-6. _Active sensors_ are sensors that generate the electromagnetic energy needed to illuminate the object that is being imaged. They broadcast energy and process returning portions of it in order to image a targeted area. Active imagery sensing capabilities include real-aperture radar, synthetic aperture radar, interferometric synthetic aperture radar, moving target indicator, and light detection and ranging systems (Pub 1.0). 

##### **PASSIVE SENSORS** 

A-7. _Passive sensors_ are sensors that use natural electromagnetic energy sources such as the Sun, naturally occurring radiation, or objects that generate heat. They generate data by capturing the reflected or emitted electromagnetic energy from an object. Passive sensors, which operate in _receive_ mode and do not emit energy, diminish the adversary’s ability to detect the fact that they are being imaged. Electro-optical sensors (except light detection and ranging) are passive (Pub 1.0).

**_Note._** While the U.S. IC tracks resolution in feet and inches, the Army converts it to meters for reporting consistency. 

###### **Radiometric Resolution** 

A-24. Radiometric resolution describes how precisely a system can represent or distinguish differences of intensity. It is usually expressed as bits or levels. For example, 8 bits or 256 levels is typical for computer image files. In theory, higher radiometric resolution results in more discernable subtle differences in intensity or reflectivity. In practice, however, effective radiometric resolution is typically limited by noise levels rather than by the number of bits represented. 

###### **Temporal Resolution** 

A-25. Temporal resolution refers to the frequency at which images are recorded. Temporal resolution for motion imagery is expressed in frames per second. 

###### **Spectral Resolution** 

A-26. Spectral resolution describes a sensor’s ability to define finite wavelength differences. The finer the spectral resolution, the narrower the wavelength range for a channel or band. Spectral resolution is determined by the number and width of bands and their location along the EMS. 

###### **Spatial Resolution** 

A-27. Spatial resolution is expressed in terms of ground sample distance for EO systems and impulse response for radar collections: 

- **Ground sample distance** refers to the specific measurements between two objects required for a sensor to distinguish them as independent objects. These measurements are limited to passive EO collection systems. 

- **Impulse response** is the term SAR uses to express measurements similar to ground sample distance. While positioning is critical to determining ground sample distances, SAR image scale and resolution are largely determined by bandwidth (wavelength) and aperture size (dwell time). 

##### **IMAGERY TYPES** 

A-28. There are two types of imagery: 

- **Still imagery** refers to an individual image of an object or location. 

- **Motion imagery** refers to a series of images collected sequentially at varying frames per second. 

###### **Still Imagery** 

A-29. Still imagery provides information about specific areas or objects at varying levels of detail. Sensors may collect still imagery in various formats, including but not limited to the following: 

- Panchromatic. • LIDAR. 

- • Polarimetric. • SAR. • Infrared. • OPIR. 

- Spectral (multispectral and hyperspectral). 

###### **_Panchromatic_** 

A-30. _Panchromatic_ uses the visible part of the electromagnetic spectrum, along with a portion of infrared spectrum, and produces an image similar to a black and white aerial photograph. The images can provide a very detailed and accurate likeness of an area or object, and can show shadows and other effects that help identify characteristics of an object or environment (Pub 1.0).

A-31. A sensor that is sensitive to all or most of the visible spectrum acquires a panchromatic or visible image. Panchromatic imagery is black and white, displayed as a grayscale image, and generally has a higher spatial resolution than infrared or spectral imagery. Panchromatic imagery can be merged with other imagery data sources. CIB is an example of a standard output format that uses panchromatic imagery. Numerous C2 systems throughout the Army use CIB (see paragraph 3-5). 

###### **_Polarimetric_** 

A-32. Polarimetric imagery measures the polarization states of energy coming from all points in a scene. This information can assist in classifying materials and identifying objects of interest for remote sensing and military applications. Polarization also provides useful information about the surface properties of an object. Fabricated objects have unnaturally smooth surfaces that emit and reflect radiation that is more polarized. Natural objects such as grass, trees, dirt, and sand emit and reflect radiation that is less polarized. 

###### **_Infrared_** 

A-33. _Infrared_ is an electro-optical phenomenology that uses the infrared part of the spectrum to detect heat and radiance/reflection, and displays that information in a gray-scale image (Pub 1.0). The different shades of gray represent differences in temperatures and emissivity of objects in the image. 

A-34. Infrared imagery comprises one of three types: 

   - **Mid-wave infrared** is used for GEOINT thermal infrared imagery. It combines infrared reflective and emissive data with the highest spatial resolution for thermal imagery. Mid-wave infrared imagery can be collected day or night, but reflectance properties may skew thermal data collected during the day. 

   - **Long-wave infrared** can be used to obtain the greatest amount of pure emittance data. Long-wave infrared imagery can be collected day or night and is not subject to reflectance properties. The spatial quality of long-wave infrared suffers substantially, however, due to the associated longer wavelengths. 

   - **Near infrared and short-wave infrared** are used to primarily identify soil and vegetation reflective properties. They are only collected during the day because the Sun or another strong illumination source needs to be apparent upon the target. 

- A-35. Applications for infrared imagery include— 

   - Characterizing foliage. 

   - Detecting camouflage. 

   - Assessing crop health. 

   - Determining a land-water boundary. 

   - Analyzing coastal hydrography. 

   - Analyzing cloud cover and cloud heights. 

   - Determining target background contrast and thermal crossover. 

**_Note._** Military applications for infrared imagery include target acquisition, surveillance, reconnaissance, night vision, homing, and tracking. 

###### **_Spectral_** 

A-36. A series of sensors sensitive to specific wavelengths (usually grouped in bands) collects spectral imagery. Adding or subtracting different bands generates color imagery. Spectral imagery is categorized into two groups mainly distinguished by the number and width of spectral bands present: multispectral and hyperspectral. 

A-37. _Multispectral imaging_ is an electro-optical phenomenology that provides a color image by using tens of additional bands of the visible and infrared portions of the spectrum. It also provides more detail to images, showing characteristics that are not otherwise visible (Pub 1.0). The bands can be combined in various ways to highlight different man-made or natural features.

A-38. _Hyperspectral imaging_ is an electro-optical phenomenology similar to multispectral imaging but uses hundreds, rather than tens, of bands of the visible and infrared portions of the electromagnetic spectrum. These extra bands can provide greater fidelity and characteristics than multispectral imaging. For example, hyperspectral imagery allows users to detect materials by their unique spectral signature (Pub 1.0). 

A-39. Spectral imaging collects multiple bands across the EMS to sense reflective and emissive values of matter within an area. The higher the band’s resolution, the more capable the sensor is of identifying specific details like rock or soil types, camouflage net manufacturers, and vegetation. Spectral imaging and reflectance value libraries provide signatures that resolve a range of matters, such as distinguishing between real aircraft and decoys, characterizing substances by various types of emissions, and detecting vehicles based on their unique signatures. 

###### **_Light Detection and Ranging_** 

A-40. _Light detection and ranging_ is an electro-optical phenomenology that uses laser pulses in the visible and infrared portions of the electromagnetic spectrum. The sensor emits a pulse of laser light toward an object, which reflects back some of the energy to the sensor. The time it takes for the energy to return provides elevation values—shorter roundtrips indicate higher elevations and longer roundtrips indicate lower elevations. This process helps measure distances to, heights of, and other characteristics of an object. The process can be used to develop three-dimensional models of an object, such as a building (Pub 1.0). LIDAR is an active sensor, similar to radar, that includes scanning and nonscanning systems capable of three-dimensional imaging. 

###### **_Synthetic Aperture Radar_** 

A-41. _Synthetic aperture radar_ is a radar phenomenology that illuminates an object with pulses of microwave energy. It applies signal processing to a series of many pulses to produce imagery (Pub 1.0). SAR imagery detects and identifies activity based on reflected radar energy. SAR systems emit and collect radar energy, recording returned energy to create a history of frequency shifts (known as a phase history) as they move in relation to the desired target. These shifts are reprocessed to create an image with consistent resolution, whether objects are closer to or farther from the sensor. Due to the sensor’s active nature, SAR imagery can illuminate objects day or night and in most weather conditions, providing tactical commanders with imagery that complements visible and infrared sensor capabilities. 

A-42. Processed radar data is depicted in a map-like format, enabling the use of unprocessed or complex radar data to collect and automatically detect motion within a single radar image and automatically detect change between two radar images. SAR imagery is nonliteral, which means it does not appear as a photo that shows an exact likeness of an object or environment. It identifies characteristics of an area or object that a literal image would not show, similar to an x ray. 

A-43. _Interferometric synthetic aperture radar_ is a phenomenology that uses a synthetic aperture radar sensor to observe a location from two separate positions. This method generates elevation data of the Earth’s surface (Pub 1.0). 

###### **_Overhead Persistent Infrared_** 

A-44. _Overhead persistent infrared_ is, within geospatial intelligence, a capability that provides on-demand, persistent, global, and/or localized coverage of high- to low-intensity infrared events to detect energy radiation from various tactical to strategic objects (JP 2-0). It is a defense program of remotely sensed data provided in large spatial samples. According to Pub 1.0, _overhead persistent infrared_ sensors operate in the visible and near infrared, short-wave infrared, and mid-wave infrared portions of the spectrum to characterize this energy as an event or process it as a scene. Overhead persistent infrared sensors provide persistent (that is, continuous) coverage of mission areas and energy radiation detection from both tactical and strategic targets including missiles launches and events, wildfires, enemy hostilities, weather, volcanos, and weapons characterization (Pub 1.0).

###### **Motion Imagery** 

A-45. Motion imagery is a valuable tool for ongoing operations and surveillance. Collecting motion imagery from sensors mounted on airborne GEOINT collection assets with long-mission endurance allows GEOINT imagery analysts to monitor high-interest activities in the OE. It also allows analysts to track moving, fleeting, and emerging targets. Near real-time FMV enables observation of rapidly developing events. 

A-46. According to Pub 1.0, in _motion geospatial intelligence_ , both electro-optical and radar sensors can create or capture motion by collecting two or more frames per second. The most common types of motion geospatial intelligence are full-motion video, moving target indicator, and wide area motion imagery (Pub 1.0). 

A-47. A motion imagery system, as described by the Motion Imagery Standards Board, is any imaging system that collects at a rate of one frame per second (1 hertz) or faster over a common field of regard. This includes but is not limited to panchromatic, infrared, multispectral, and hyperspectral systems. Motion imagery, as with still imagery, is collected in various portions of the EMS. 

**_Note._** The Motion Imagery Standards Board is the official standards body responsible for reviewing and recommending standards for motion imagery, associated metadata, audio, and other related systems for use within the NSG and DOD. The Motion Imagery Standards Board makes no formal distinction between the terms motion imagery and FMV. Some entities call video with no metadata FMV, but motion imagery must contain metadata. Historically, FMV has been the subset of motion imagery at television-like frame rates (24 to 60 hertz). (Obtain information on the Motion Imagery Standards Board through the Geospatial-Intelligence Standards Working Group website on NIPRNET.) 

###### **_Full-Motion Video_** 

A-48. _Full-motion video_ , typically an electro-optical capability, uses many still frames per second, creating a near-real-time video capability, which may be displayed as visible or infrared and may be recorded by digital or analog means. This near-real time capability allows observation of rapidly developing events and is a valuable tool for ongoing operations. It is also useful for continuous observation of an object to analyze its patterns over a long period of time (Pub 1.0). FMV enables commanders to make decisions and execute missions from a safe distance without endangering their troops. Aerial GEOINT collection assets can transmit a direct video feed to a nearby ground control station or broadcast video via satellite to command centers around the globe. They provide an on-demand, near real-time, close-up view of the combat zone that would not otherwise be possible. 

###### **_Moving Target Indicator_** 

A-49. _Moving target indicator_ is an electro-optical and radar capability that shows only those objects that are in motion, and provides information on the speed and direction that objects are moving (Pub 1.0). This capability provides commanders with better situational awareness of the AO. 

- A-50. MTI imagery quality considerations include target range resolution and temporal resolution: 

   - **Target range resolution** is sometimes referred to as high-range resolution in the case of MTI imagery. It determines whether two or more targets moving in proximity will be detected as individual targets. 

   - **Temporal resolution** refers to the frequency with which a radar beam passes over an area. Frequent revisits allow radar to achieve track continuity and increase the likelihood of target detection by reducing the probability of obscuration from screening by trees, buildings, or other objects. 

###### **_Wide Area Motion Imagery_** 

A-51. _Wide area motion imagery_ is a set of motion imagery sensors with a wide field of view diameter of up to 8 kilometers, which provides persistent surveillance of a broad area of coverage. Wide area motion imagery data enables forensic backtracking of kinetic events, network analysis, pattern of life studies, and security monitoring (Pub 1.0). Wide area motion imagery (WAMI) also refers to a surveillance, reconnaissance, and

### **Policy Considerations** 

#### **OVERVIEW** 

B-1. Performing GEOINT operations may require various types of approvals depending on location, mission, organization, or other aspects of the OE. As the functional manager for GEOINT, NGA will typically manage GEOINT mission approval based on current policies. Any GEOINT operation requiring approval from NGA includes a formal request process that must be vetted and validated. 

#### **DOMESTIC IMAGERY REQUIREMENTS** 

B-2. When requesting domestic imagery from NGA for purposes other than intelligence, NSG members must justify their request based on identified mission requirements. The subsequent use of such imagery for intelligence purposes requires an NDL account (see paragraph 3-66), obtained through the ADRO, which manages Army domestic GEOINT policies and access (see paragraph 2-19). 

##### **DOMESTIC IMAGERY REQUEST** 

B-3. Domestic imagery collection or access requests should include a pertinent justification that— 

- Provides a description of the requirement and the geolocation of the requested areas (including a concept of operations, if possible). 

- Identifies exempted mission area being supported. 

- Identifies mission duration with a start and end date. 

- Specifies whether the mission requires temporary or ongoing access. 

- Provides NDL account user information. 

- Identifies the classification level of network access the user requires. 

##### **DOMESTIC IMAGERY RELEASE DOCUMENT** 

B-4. A domestic imagery release document is required when Army intelligence functions obtain imagery of private property. The property owner may be an individual or a city. The owner or chief executive of a municipality must sign the release. 

B-5. A private property release statement should include a statement of consent granting the U.S. Government permission to take overhead photographs and collect remote sensing data of specific targets and surrounding area. The statement should also include the following: the landowner’s name, a list of the specific targets, the full address of the location, a statement of understanding that photography and data collection will occur on specific dates, and the landowner’s signature.

##### **In this publication—** 

- _Collection asset_ is a collection system, platform, or capability that is supporting, assigned to, or attached to a particular commander (JP 2-0). In many contexts, _collection assets_ are referred to as intelligence assets, intelligence collection assets, or information collection assets to describe units, systems, and sensors that perform information collection. 

- A _collection resource_ refers to a collection system, platform, or capability that is not assigned or attached to a specific commander, unit, or echelon and is requested and coordinated through the chain of command of the unit that directs and controls them. A collection asset is subordinate to the requesting unit or echelon, while a collection resource is not.

C-2. ### **GEOINT Support to Targeting** 

#### **OVERVIEW** 

D-1. _Targeting_ is the process of selecting and prioritizing targets and matching the appropriate response to them, considering operational requirements and capabilities (JP 3-0). The Army targeting process applies the decide, detect, deliver, and assess (D3A) methodology to facilitate the engagement of the right target with the right asset at the right time. (See FM 3-60 for doctrine on the D3A methodology.) 

D-2. GEOINT supports targeting throughout the D3A methodology through analysis and production during the decide, detect, and assess functions by identifying and nominating potential targets and assessing postoperational results. GEOINT monitors deliberate strikes during the deliver function. Accurately identifying and analyzing activities and assessing battle damage in the AO can significantly affect the COA selected by the commander. GEOINT shapes the commander’s decision-making process by providing a valuable tool for visualizing and analyzing threat COAs. 

#### **DECIDE** 

D-3. The decide function begins the targeting process. During this function, the commander and staff use their knowledge of the enemy along with the results of IPOE to determine targeting priorities. The GEOINT cell supports this effort by developing IPOE products and refining and updating products throughout each function of the operation. 

D-4. GEOINT products focus on the effects of terrain on friendly and enemy COAs. GEOINT analysis identifies enemy TTP and how the enemy may use the terrain to their advantage. GEOINT also aids in identifying and characterizing HPTs and HVTs through imagery exploitation. 

D-5. GEOINT input to the information collection plan is vital to the decide function. While GEOINT collection assets engage with targets during the detect function, successful targeting depends on ensuring the appropriate GEOINT capability is selected to collect in the correct location at the proper time. In addition to supporting the initial information collection plan, GEOINT production and planning to support HVT and HPT lists influences decision making related to the collection and engagement of targets. 

D-6. During competition below armed conflict, targeting continuously refines the decide function. Priority targets must remain valid throughout the target development process to ensure they are authorized for engagement if a transition to armed conflict occurs. Target development occurs in three stages: basic, intermediate, and advanced. The intelligence staff, with significant GEOINT support, provides input at each stage. (CJCSI 3370.01D provides more information on target development.) 

##### **BASIC TARGET DEVELOPMENT** 

D-7. Basic target development involves identifying a target, providing its location, and determining its primary function: 

- **Target identification** provides the name and unique identifier for the target. 

- **Target location** provides the coordinates for where the target is located and distance and direction to other targets within the area. 

- **Target function** establishes the primary function of the target (what it was created to do). 

D-8. GEOINT provides products that enable visualization of target locations. GEOINT analysis and patternof-life support assessments determine a target’s function. 

##### **INTERMEDIATE TARGET DEVELOPMENT** 

- D-9. Intermediate target development provides in-depth information about a target: 

   - **Target significance** addresses why a particular target is important to the overall target system. 

   - **Target description** provides a physical description of the recognizable attributes of the target.

**GEOINT Support to Targeting** 

#### **DETECT** 

D-14. The detect function focuses on detecting and tracking HPTs identified during the decide function of D3A through the implementation of the information collection plan. The detect function for GEOINT involves exploiting data from GEOINT collection assets tasked to support the targeting process and recommending new collection based on that exploitation. As GEOINT imagery analysts exploit data from GEOINT collection assets within tasked NAIs, they may identify unexpected enemy activity that meets targeting criteria. 

D-15. Target tracking is inherent to detection. Once a target is identified, GEOINT data is used to track the target until effects are levied. GEOINT supports tracking mobile targets via FMV, MTI, two-color multiview, and change detection analysis. FMV and MTI analysis can track a target’s movement in real time, while change detection and two-color multiview provide information concerning changes in vehicle or equipment location. These capabilities assist the G-2/S-2 in developing estimates of the enemy target’s location as they prepare to engage the target during the deliver function. 

#### **DELIVER** 

D-16. The deliver function executes the target engagement guidance developed during the decide function. This function requires decisions concerning the time of engagement, desired effect, which asset to use, and potential for collateral damage. GEOINT supports this effort by providing precise target mensuration to reduce target location error. 

D-17. During target engagement, analysts monitor live FMV feeds to provide analysis of the target and its surroundings. The analysis conducted during this stage requires continuous tracking of the target to maintain positive identification, addresses potential targets of opportunity, and should include considerations of the environment as targets move. This assists the commander in engaging targets within the rules of engagement and mitigating the effects of collateral damage. 

#### **ASSESS** 

D-18. _Combat assessment_ is the determination of the effectiveness of friendly attack (JP 3-60). It comprises BDA, munitions effectiveness assessment, and reengagement recommendation. The GEOINT cell analyzes imagery and develops BDA products to— 

- Aid in assessing the effectiveness of weapon systems. 

- Determine physical and functional damage. 

- Inform the commander’s decision regarding potential reengagement of a target. 

D-19. BDA consists of three types of assessments: physical damage, functional damage, and target system. GEOINT is instrumental in assessing physical damage, as this assessment requires interpreting damage based on visual analysis of the target post engagement. Physical damage assessment lays the groundwork for functional damage assessment, which informs target system assessment. 

##### **PHYSICAL DAMAGE ASSESSMENT** 

D-20. _Physical damage assessment_ is the estimate of the quantitative extent of physical damage to a target (JP 3-60). The GEOINT imagery analyst assists the commander and staff in determining physical damage to a target via exploitation of imagery collected before, during, and after engagement. The analyst reviews previous BDA reports and considers the relationship between the target and the munition used to engage it as they make their physical damage assessment. The analyst identifies additional or collateral damage resulting from the operation, and any denial and deception activities observed. 

D-21. The GEOINT cell prepares a physical damage assessment report and imagery interpretation report for each target engaged. As they do so, they must also determine at what confidence level they can assess the damage.

##### **FUNCTIONAL DAMAGE ASSESSMENT**

GEOINT reporting and analysis may assist in identifying functional damage, recuperation times, and reattack recommendations for the BDA target system assessment report.

### **GEOINT Integrated Architecture** 

#### **ARMY GEOINT ARCHITECTURE** 

E-1. The GEOINT integrated architecture is a vast net-centric environment, a framework for full human and technical connectivity and interoperability, allowing DOD users and mission partners to share the information they need, when they need it, and in forms they can understand and act on with confidence. Additionally, the net-centric environment protects access to information, via individual network adjudication processes, against those who should not have it. 

**_Note._** The _Department of Defense information network_ is the information capabilities and associated processes owned or leased by the Department of Defense for collecting, processing, storing, disseminating, and managing information, whether interconnected or stand-alone. (JP 6-0). 

E-2. The Army’s reachback concept of support is critical to the Nation’s ability to build a campaign-quality force with joint and expeditionary capabilities. Within a modular force design, GEOINT imagery analysts examine imagery received from a multitude of GEOINT collection assets—from national satellite systems to tactical UASs—to answer commanders’ PIRs and information requirements. 

E-3. GEOINT imagery analysts within corps main and tactical command posts receive, process, exploit, and disseminate GEOINT products to support current corps operations and to provide direct support to division, combat aviation brigade, and BCT operations.

##### **SECTION II – TERMS** 

###### **avenue of approach** 

A path used by an attacking force leading to its objective or to key terrain. Avenues of approach exist in all domains. (ADP 3-90) 

###### **collection** 

In intelligence usage, the acquisition of information and the provision of this information to processing elements. (JP 2-0)

**Glossary** 

###### **collection asset** 

A collection system, platform, or capability that is supporting, assigned to, or attached to a particular commander. (JP 2-0) 

###### **collection operations management** 

The authoritative direction, scheduling, and control of specific collection operations and associated processing, exploitation, and reporting resources. (JP 2-0) 

###### **collection requirements management** 

The authoritative development and control of collection, processing, exploitation, and/or reporting requirements that normally result in the direct tasking of requirements to units over which the commander has authority, or the generation of tasking requests to collection management authorities at a higher, lower, or lateral echelon to accomplish the collection mission. (JP 2-0) 

###### **combat assessment** 

The determination of the effectiveness of friendly attack. (JP 3-60) 

###### **common operational picture** 

(DOD) A single, identical display of relevant information shared by more than one command that facilitates collaborative planning and assists all echelons to achieve situational awareness. (JP 3-0) 

###### **concealment** 

Protection from observation or surveillance. (FM 3-96) 

###### **cover** 

(Army) Protection from the effects of fires. (FM 3-96) 

###### **crisis** 

An emerging incident or situation involving a possible threat to the United States, its citizens, military forces, or vital interests that develops rapidly and creates a condition of such diplomatic, economic, or military importance that commitment of military forces and resources is contemplated to achieve national and/or strategic objectives. (JP 3-0) 

###### **decisive terrain** 

Key terrain whose seizure and retention is mandatory for successful mission accomplishment. (ADP 3-90) 

###### **Department of Defense information network** 

The information capabilities and associated processes owned or leased by the Department of Defense for collecting, processing, storing, disseminating, and managing information, whether interconnected or stand-alone. (JP 6-0) 

###### **dissemination** 

In intelligence usage, the delivery of intelligence to users in a suitable form. (JP 2-0) 

###### **field of fire** 

The area that a weapon or group of weapons may cover effectively from a given position. (FM 3-90) 

###### **functional damage assessment** 

The estimate of the effect of military force to degrade or destroy the functional or operational capability of the target to perform its intended mission and on the level of success in achieving operational objectives established against the target. (JP 3-60) 

###### **geospatial information** 

(DOD) Information that identifies the geographic location and characteristics of natural or constructed features and boundaries on or about the Earth, including: data and information derived from, among other things, remote sensing, mapping, and surveying technologies; and mapping, charting, geomatics data, and related products and services. (JP 2-0)

**Glossary** 

###### **geospatial information and services** 

The collection, information extraction, storage, dissemination, and exploitation of geodetic, geomagnetic, imagery (both commercial and national source), gravimetric, aeronautical, topographic, hydrographic, littoral, cultural, and toponymic data accurately referenced to a precise location on the surface of the Earth. (JP 2-0) 

###### **geospatial intelligence** 

The exploitation and analysis of imagery and geospatial information to describe, assess, and visually depict physical features and geographically referenced activities on or about the Earth. Geospatial intelligence consists of imagery, imagery intelligence, and geospatial information. (JP 2-0) 

###### **imagery** 

A likeness or presentation of any natural or man-made feature or related object or activity, and the positional data acquired at the same time the likeness or representation was acquired, including: products produced by space-based national intelligence reconnaissance systems; and likeness and presentations produced by satellites, airborne platforms, unmanned aerial vehicles, or other similar means (except that such term does not include handheld or clandestine photography taken by or on behalf of human intelligence collection organizations). (JP 2-0) 

###### **imagery intelligence** 

The technical, geographic, and intelligence information derived through the interpretation or analysis of imagery and collateral materials. (JP 2-0) 

###### **intelligence production** 

The integration, evaluation, analysis, and interpretation of information from single or multiple sources into finished intelligence for known or anticipated military and related national security consumer requirements. (JP 2-0) 

###### **joint deployable intelligence support system** 

A transportable workstation and communications suite that electronically extends a joint intelligence center to a joint task force or other tactical user. (JP 2-0) 

###### **key terrain** 

(Army) An identifiable characteristic whose seizure or retention affords a marked advantage to either combatant. (ADP 3-90) 

###### **multinational operations** 

A collective term to describe military actions conducted by forces of two or more nations, usually undertaken within the structure of a coalition or alliance. (JP 3-16) 

###### **National System for Geospatial Intelligence** 

The combination of technology, policies, capabilities, doctrine, activities, people, data, and organizations necessary to produce geospatial intelligence in an integrated, multi-intelligence environment. (JP 2-0) 

###### **observation** 

The condition of weather and terrain that permits a force to see the friendly, enemy, and neutral personnel and systems, and key aspects of the environment. (FM 1-02.1) 

###### **obstacle** 

Any barrier designed or employed to disrupt, fix, turn, or block the movement and maneuver, and to impose additional losses in personnel, time, and equipment. (JP 3-15) 

###### **overhead persistent infrared** 

Within geospatial intelligence, a capability that provides on-demand, persistent, global, and/or localized coverage of high- to low-intensity infrared events to detect energy radiation from various tactical to strategic objects. (JP 2-0) 

###### **physical damage assessment** 

The estimate of the quantitative extent of physical damage to a target. (JP 3-60)

###### **processing** 

A system of operations designed to convert raw data into useful information. (JP 2-0) 

###### **request for information** 

Any specific, time-sensitive, ad hoc requirement for intelligence information or products to support an ongoing crisis or operation not necessarily related to standing requirements or scheduled intelligence production. (JP 2-0) 

###### **target system assessment** 

The broad assessment of the overall impact and effectiveness of military force applied against the operation of an enemy target system, significant subdivisions of the system, or total combat effectiveness relative to the operational objectives established. (JP 3-60) 

###### **targeting** 

The process of selecting and prioritizing targets and matching the appropriate response to them, considering operational requirements and capabilities. (JP 3-0)

(JP 2-0)
