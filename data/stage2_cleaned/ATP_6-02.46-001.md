### **Operational Environment and Threat** 

3 This chapter discusses the operational environment and threat. Section I 4 outlines the contested and congested nature of the operational environment. 5 Section II discusses the various types of state and non-state threat actors 6 who pose risks to expeditionary signal companies. 

7 **UNDERSTANDING THE ENVIRONMENT** 

8 1-1. Factors that affect operations extend far beyond the boundaries of a commander’s 9 assigned area. An _operational environment_ is the aggregate of the conditions, 10 circumstances, and influences that affect the employment of capabilities and bear on the 11 decisions of the commander (JP 3-0). An operational environment encompasses physical 12 areas of air, land, maritime, space and cyberspace domains. 

- 13 1-2. For the Army, the _cyberspace domain_ is the interdependent networks of information 14 technology infrastructures and resident data, including the Internet, telecommunication 15 networks, computer systems, embedded processors and controllers, and relevant 16 portions of the electromagnetic spectrum (FM 3-0). 

17 1-3. Understanding the operational environment is essential for signal leaders, 18 engineers, planners, system operators, and cybersecurity professionals to plan and 19 execute effective signal support. _The Department of Defense information network_ is the 20 set of information capabilities and associated processes for collecting, processing, 21 storing, disseminating, and managing information on demand to warfighters, policy 22 makers, and support personnel, whether interconnected or stand-alone (JP 6-0). 

- 23 1-4. Various characteristics of an operational environment impact a unit’s ability to 24 operate and communicate. Leaders should consider the following aspects of the 25 operational environment when planning and conducting operations: 26 • Congested and contested electromagnetic spectrum. 27 • Threats. 28 • Vulnerabilities. 29 • Risks. 30 • Hazards. 

- 31 **CONGESTED ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM** 

32 1-5. Expeditionary signal companies operate in a congested environment. The extensive 33 use of the electromagnetic spectrum creates competition for bandwidth resources. All 34 forces and supporting agencies depend on access to the electromagnetic spectrum for 35 communications, information collection, and electromagnetic warfare (EW) 36 capabilities. Signal systems rely on the electromagnetic spectrum for network transport. 37 _Network transport_ is the processes, equipment, and transmission media that provide 38 connectivity and move data between networking devices and facilities (FM 6-02). For 39 this reason, gaining and maintaining access to the electromagnetic spectrum is critical **Chapter 1**

40 for signal support to joint and Army operations. Refer to ATP 6-02.70 for more 41 information about spectrum management operations. 

- 42 **CONTESTED ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM** 

- 43 1-6. Expeditionary signal companies also operate in a contested environment. The 44 expeditionary signal battalion or corps signal brigade S-3 plans and delivers a robust 45 communications network to mitigate the effects of a contested environment. Contested 46 communications environments exist due to threat actions to degrade or deny access to 47 the electromagnetic spectrum within the assigned area. See appendix A for more 48 information on tactics, techniques, and procedures to recognize and respond to threat 49 effects in a contested environment. 

- 50 1-7. Expeditionary signal company elements should be familiar with and implement 51 electromagnetic protection techniques to enhance survivability. _Electromagnetic_ 52 _protection_ is the division of EW involving actions taken to protect personnel, facilities, 53 and equipment from any effects of friendly or enemy use of the electromagnetic 54 spectrum that degrade, neutralize, or destroy friendly combat capability (JP 3-85). 55 Enemy attacks may combine electromagnetic attacks, other information warfare effects, 56 or lethal fires to deny friendly forces the use of spectrum-dependent systems. To 57 accomplish this goal, threat forces gather technical and combat information about 58 friendly forces. As enemy forces locate and identify friendly units, enemy information 59 warfare elements establish priorities to— 

- 60 • Jam communications assets. 

- 61 • Deceptively enter radio networks. 62 • Interfere with the normal flow of U.S. and allied communications. 

- 63 • Target for lethal fires.

#### **THREATS** 

- 65 1-8. A _threat_ is any combination of actors, entities, or forces that have the capability and 66 intent to harm United States forces, United States national interests, or the homeland 67 (ADP 3-0). Threats can be categorized as adversaries, enemies, or insider threats. An 68 _adversary_ is a party acknowledged as potentially hostile to a friendly party and against 69 which the use of force may be envisaged (JP 3-0). An _enemy_ is a party identified as 70 hostile against which the use of force is authorized (ADP 3-0). Insider threats present 71 unique challenges because they are trusted individuals with access to Army capabilities 72 and sensitive operational information. Threats may include individuals or groups, 73 organized, or not organized. 

- 74 1-9. Threats are a fundamental part of an overall operational environment. A threat can 75 disrupt, destroy, or intercept the flow of information while operating from the other side 76 of the world. Threats use an array of capabilities and methods to threaten Army systems 77 and networks. 

78 **PEER THREAT** 

79 1-10. A peer threat is an adversary or enemy with capabilities and capacity to oppose 80 United States forces across multiple domains worldwide or in a specific region where 81 they enjoy a position of relative advantage. Unlike the previous two decades of 82 counterinsurgency, peer threats will actively target and prioritize communications assets **Operational Environment and Threat**

83 on the battlefield during combat. Peer threats understand that without use of cyberspace 84 and space domains, the Army loses its advantage during competition below the level of 85 armed conflict, during crisis, and in armed conflict. 

86 1-11. Peer threats can employ resources across multiple domains to create lethal and 87 nonlethal effects throughout an operational environment (ADP 3-0). Peer threats 88 generate tactical, operational, and theater strategic-level challenges that may be orders 89 of magnitude more challenging than the previous two decades of counter insurgency 90 operations. 

- 91 1-12. Peer threats have the capability to degrade, disrupt, or destroy communication 92 systems an expeditionary signal company employs through lethal fires, EW, and 93 malicious cyberspace activities. An expeditionary signal company communications 94 backbone supports the joint force’s ability to carry out operations at echelons above 95 brigade. Communications disruptions can severely hinder the commander’s ability to 96 employ the joint force. 

- 97 Soldiers assigned to an expeditionary signal company need to understand what 98 capabilities peer threats can employ to degrade or destroy their networks. Those 99 capabilities are— 

- 100 • Jamming and exploitation. 

- 101 • Malicious cyberspace activities. 

- 102 • Direct and indirect lethal fires.

##### **HYBRID THREAT** 

- 104 1-13. Hybrid threat is the diverse and dynamic combination of regular forces, irregular 105 forces, terrorists, or criminal elements unified to achieve mutually benefitting effects 106 (ADP 3-0). Some aspects of the hybrid threat, such as criminal or terrorist organizations, 107 do not abide by the law of armed conflict. Hybrid threats seek to exploit asymmetric 108 advantages over an adversary to avoid engaging in direct combat. 

- 109 1-14. The hybrid threat understands that the most challenging environment for the U.S. 110 forces is one where conventional military operations occur in concert with irregular 111 warfare. Enemies may employ malicious cyberspace activities and exploitation, 112 battlefield jammers, and space-based capabilities, such as anti-satellite weapons, to 113 disrupt U.S. communications; positioning, navigation, and timing; synchronization; and 114 freedom of maneuver. The hybrid threat concept is not only making do with what is 115 available, but deliberately taking advantage of all tools at hand to create a complex 116 operational environment. Refer to FM 6-02 for more information about hybrid threat 117 effects on signal support.

###### **_Large Network Team_** 

207 **_Large Network Team_** 208 2-31. The large network team is responsible for the installation, operation, and operator209 level maintenance of the Single Shelter Switch. The Single Shelter Switch is co-located 210 with the expeditionary signal battalion headquarters and headquarters company at a 211 large command post of up to 500 end users, and functions as the primary 212 communications node for the expeditionary signal battalion’s portion of the tactical 213 network. The corps signal brigade or theater signal brigade S-3 can use a Single Shelter 214 Switch to establish a network operations and security center, as well as plan and engineer 215 the local area network. A Single Shelter Switch deploys to establish a network 216 communications node for a large command post or command post cluster. 217 2-32. A large network team is responsible for installing, operating, maintaining, and 218 performing operator-level maintenance of a High Capacity Line of Sight (HCLOS) V(3) 219 or Terrestrial Transmission Line of Sight (TRILOS) Radio. All HCLOS are replaced in 220 the objective table of organization and equipment by the TRILOS. Units who have not 221 been fielded TRILOS will continue operating the HCLOS until fielding is complete. 222 2-33. The TRILOS provides a substantial increase in bandwidth in comparison to the 223 HCLOS radio system, with a significant reduction in size, weight, and power. TRILOS 224 allows the ability to transport up to three line of sight transmissions from one antenna. 225 The TRILOS (V)1 can transport up to six line of sight transmissions from two antennas 226 attached to one antenna base. The TRILOS (V)2 can transport up to three line of sight 227 transmissions from one antenna. The TRILOS can operate in a satellite denied 228 environment. The TRILOS can transmit and receive three separate line of sight 229 transmissions with one antenna mast. The TRILOS allows line of sight transmissions 230 between communications nodes throughout the theater. The TRILOS small form factor 231 provides a significant reduction in size, weight, and power compared to the HCLOS 232 radio. 

- 233 2-34. The HCLOS V(3) is the primary means of data transport between the Single 234 Shelter Switch and its associated Command Post Nodes, using terrestrial microwave 235 radio systems capable of up to 16 megabits per second of data throughput. The HCLOS 236 V(3) allows greater bandwidth than the Satellite Transportable Terminal, but is limited 237 to line of sight distance—approximately 40 kilometers (25 miles). 238 2-35. #### **MEASURES TO PREVENT THREAT EFFECTS** 

25 A-3. Understanding threat capabilities is critical to hardening the deployed network 26 against enemy EW and signals intelligence. If an enemy cannot detect command posts 27 and communications sites, it cannot target the sites with electromagnetic attack or lethal 28 fires.

##### **LIMITING ELECTROMAGNETIC SIGNATURE** 

- 30 A-4. To protect against a peer threat’s ability to locate and target radio signals, the S-3 31 must plan signal support in a way that limits the electromagnetic signature of command 32 posts. Measures to reduce the electromagnetic signature of command posts and 33 communications sites include— 

- 34 • Careful siting of radio equipment. 

- 35 • Employment of directional antennas. 

- 36 • Operations using lowest power required. 

- 37 • Limiting radio transmissions. 

- 38 • Using a random schedule. 

39 A-5. Electromagnetic protection techniques can also help mask the electromagnetic 40 signature. The cyber electromagnetic warfare officer can assist the S-3 in planning 41 electromagnetic protection measures to reduce the command post electromagnetic 42 signature. 

- 43 **TERRAIN MASKING** 

44 A-6. Terrain masking can effectively block radio signals from reaching enemy 45 direction-finding capabilities. Positioning communications systems with large terrain 46 features or manmade structures between the communications system and the forward 47 line of own troops effectively blocks an enemy from detecting the signal. 

- 48 **CAMOUFLAGE NET MASKING** 

- 49 A-7. Radar reflective camouflage netting is an effective means of blocking stray 50 electromagnetic radiation from directional antennas. Camouflage netting placed to the 51 sides and back of a line of sight or SATCOM antenna ensures only the main beam of 52 the antenna radiates. Since the main beam is directional, it is much harder for the enemy 53 to detect; the enemy would need to be directly in the transmission path. 

- 54 **LINE OF SIGHT** 

- 55 A-8. High-throughput line of sight radios can carry high bandwidth data over distances 56 up to 40 kilometers (25 miles). Planners should engineer links to minimize chances of 57 detection, targeting, and jamming. If the line of sight path is parallel to the forward line 58 of own troops, an enemy is less likely to detect the signal, and enemy jammers cannot 59 reach the antenna with a strong enough signal to jam the radio. 

- 60 **REMOTE ANTENNAS** 

- 61 A-9. Large command posts and high-throughput communications systems emit a 62 significant amount of electromagnetic energy. Planners and operators can mask some of 63 this energy with directional antennas, site selection, terrain masking techniques, but 64 some energy remains. Because command and control capabilities are a priority target 65 for peer threats, anything near the communications system is at risk of destruction from 66 lethal fires. 

- 67 A-10. Commanders and signal planners should consider locating major communications 68 assemblages as far from the supported command post as practical. Selecting a site with 69 terrain features, manmade structures, or distance between communications systems and 70 command posts protects the command post from lethal fires. Commanders and planners 71 must consider the additional physical security and site defense requirements for a remote 72 site during planning. 

- 73 **FREQUENT COMMAND POST DISPLACEMENT** 

- 74 A-11. Despite efforts to reduce and mask the electromagnetic signature of a command 75 post, a peer threat may eventually locate it. Moving a command post frequently reduces 76 its chance of destruction. This is especially important when operating within the range 77 of enemy long range fires. Peer threats are able to mass lethal fires to destroy an area 78 target quickly. During training exercises, units must practice frequent command post 79 displacement and handover between the main and tactical command posts. 

80 A-12. Leaders should focus command posts on the minimum functions necessary to 81 retain their mobility and do everything possible to avoid detection. When the risk of 82 enemy fires is high, commanders consider making their operations more decentralized, 83 dispersing command post nodes into smaller component nodes, and greater dispersion 84 of electromagnetic signatures (FM 3-0). 

- 85 **SATELLITE SITE SELECTION AND RADIO FREQUENCY INTERFERENCE** 

- 86 A-13. SATCOM systems suffer from natural and manmade interference. Natural 87 sources of interference include solar activity, dense vegetation, thick cloud cover, heavy 88 precipitation, and sandstorms. Manmade sources of interference include vehicles, 89 buildings, and other structures that obstruct the line of sight path to the satellite, 90 jamming, and radio frequency interference. 

- 91 A-14. Commanders and staffs need to identify and minimize the command post footprint 92 and electromagnetic signature. Planners and operators should identify physical 93 surroundings to minimize interference and mask the electromagnetic signatures of 94 deployed communications systems. Refer to ATP 6-02.70 for more information on radio 95 frequency interference. 

- 96 A-15. Signal elements operating from locations without masking terrain features or 97 structures can use power settings and radio frequency management techniques, to 98 control interference and detection. When establishing satellite links, the equipment 99 operator should communicate with satellite network controllers to determine the 

- 100 minimum required power level. Using a higher power signal than needed for 101 communications increases the likelihood an enemy detection and targeting of the 102 communications capability. Refer to ATP 6-02.53 and ATP 3-12.3 for more information 103 on masking and power settings. 

- 104 **RECOGNIZING AND RESPONDING TO THREAT** 105 **EFFECTS** 

- 106 A-16. Because peer threats consider friendly command and control capabilities to be 107 high priority targets, they have developed capabilities to deny their enemies the effective 108 use of the electromagnetic spectrum for communications. Signal Soldiers and leaders 109 must learn to identify and respond to threat effects in cyberspace and the electromagnetic 110 spectrum. 

- 111 **ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM (JAMMING)** 

- 112 A-17. _Electromagnetic jamming_ is deliberate radiation, reradiation, or reflection of 113 electromagnetic energy for the purpose of preventing or reducing an enemy’s effective 114 use of the electromagnetic spectrum, with the intent of degrading or neutralizing the 115 enemy’s combat capability (JP 3-85). Jamming is an effective way for the enemy to 116 disrupt friendly communications. An enemy only needs a transmitter tuned to a U.S. 117 frequency with enough power to overpower friendly signals to effectively jam U.S. 118 systems. 

- 119 A-18. Jammers operate against receivers, not transmitters. The two modes of jamming 120 are spot and barrage jamming. Spot jamming concentrates power on one channel or 121 frequency. Barrage jamming is power spread over several frequencies or channels at the 122 same time. It is important to recognize jamming, but it can be difficult to detect. Refer 123 to ATP 3-12.3 and ATP 6-02.53 for more information about electromagnetic jamming.

###### **Attacks Against Personal Electronic Devices** 

|549|A-73. Widespread use of personal electronic devices creates significant vulnerabilities|
|---|---|
|550|when operating against a peer threat. Peer threats have demonstrated advanced|
|551|capabilities to exploit personal electronic devices, seize control of cellular|
|552|communications networks, and locate personal cellular phones with precision. This|
|553|gives them the ability to collect information, conduct information warfare activities, and|
|554|direct accurate lethal fires. Any personal electronic device capable of connecting to Wi-|
|555|Fi, Bluetooth, or cellular communications systems is vulnerable to attack.|

###### **_Preventive Measures_** 

- 556 **_Preventive Measures_** 557 A-74. Measures to protect against personal electronic device attacks include— 558 • Maintaining strict control and accountability of personal electronic devices. 559 • Downloading only trusted apps from approved sources. 560 • Maintaining current security updates on devices and apps. 561 • Disabling Bluetooth and Wi-Fi features when they are not in use. 562 • Encrypting sensitive files and personal information. 563 • Allowing only government-provided personal electronic devices to connect to 564 the DOD network. 

- 565 • Commanders should consider restricting or banning the use of personal 566 electronic devices, based on the tactical situation. 

###### **_Indicators_** 

- 567 **_Indicators_** 568 A-75. Some possible indicators of attacks against personal electronic devices are— 569 • Enemy attacks that seem to correlate with personal electronic device usage. 570 • Incoming lethal fires that occur with unexplained precision. 571 • Receiving a barrage of text messages—up to several per second—preventing 572 the intended use of the device. 

- 573 • Incoming propaganda or psychological warfare messages from unknown 574 numbers. 

- 575 **_Reaction_** 

- 576 A-76. If a unit suspects it has come under attack, commanders should— 577 • Quickly displace the element under attack and direct all personnel to turn off 578 personal electronic devices. 579 • Disable personal electronic devices and confiscate them, if necessary. 580 • Report to the next higher echelon.
